**Trend Analysis:** UPSC is moving decisively toward application-and-threshold testing — the question is rarely "define this term" but rather "which of these statements about how this instrument works is correct," demanding operational understanding over label-recognition. The 2025 paper's volume surge to 17 questions — highest in this window — suggests economics is being weighted more heavily, not less, going into 2026.

## How UPSC Tests Economics

UPSC economics questions test four cognitive operations that are distinct from university-level economics: **threshold identification**, **mechanism directionality**, **boundary classification**, and **institutional attribution**.

Threshold identification means knowing the exact condition at which a rule activates — not that FRBM sets deficit limits, but what the specific statutory number is and which deficit it applies to. Mechanism directionality means knowing which way an instrument moves an outcome: an OMO purchase *increases* liquidity, a CRR hike *reduces* it — statement-based questions are almost always built around reversing one of these arrows and asking if you notice. Boundary classification means knowing which side of a line something falls on: is this receipt capital or revenue, is this market money or capital, is this NBFC in the middle or upper layer? Most wrong answers in UPSC economics come from candidates who know the categories but have never drilled the boundary case. Institutional attribution means knowing *who* has the legal authority or functional responsibility for a specific action — RBI versus SEBI versus IRDAI, or Centre versus State versus Finance Commission.

What this demands in practice is that you study economics from instrument-level notes rather than chapter summaries: for every policy tool, write one line on what it does, which direction, to whom, under whose authority, and at what threshold it triggers. ### MSP Computation

The Minimum Support Price (MSP) is a price floor announced by the Union Cabinet on the recommendation of the Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices (CACP), a statutory body under the Ministry of Agriculture. CACP evaluates three cost formulas: **A2** (actual paid-out costs — seeds, fertilisers, hired labour, irrigation, lease rent paid), **A2+FL** (A2 plus imputed value of family labour), and **C2** (A2+FL plus imputed rent of owned land and interest on owned fixed capital — the most comprehensive). Since 2018-19, the government guarantees MSP at **minimum 1.5× A2+FL cost** (popularly called "50% return over cost"), though farmer organisations demand 1.5× C2. MSP covers **22 mandated crops**: 14 kharif (paddy, jowar, bajra, maize, ragi, arhar, moong, urad, cotton, groundnut, sunflower, soybean, sesame, nigerseed), 6 rabi (wheat, barley, gram, masur, rapeseed/mustard, safflower), and 2 commercial crops (jute, copra); **turmeric and other spices are NOT part of the 22 MSP crops** covered under CACP's mandatory review. The 2025 PYQ on turmeric directly tested whether CACP recommends MSP for spice crops and whether Cabinet alone announces without CACP — making the precise institutional sequence (CACP recommends → CCEA/Cabinet announces) and crop coverage critical knowledge.

**UPSC Traps:**
- The PYQ exploits the misconception that CACP recommends MSP for all agricultural crops including turmeric/spices — in reality, CACP's mandatory MSP coverage is limited to 22 specific crops, and turmeric falls outside this list despite being an important commercial crop.
- The PYQ tests whether students confuse "CACP recommends" with "Cabinet announces independently" — the institutional sequence is non-negotiable: CACP recommendation precedes Cabinet (CCEA) announcement, and conflating these two steps as interchangeable is a classic trap.
- Students often treat A2+FL and C2 as equivalent or assume the 50% return guarantee is over C2 — the government's legal commitment is over A2+FL only; C2-based 50% return remains a demand, not policy, and mixing these makes statement-based options incorrect.

**Question format:** Statement-based (2–3 statements, "which are correct"), testing the institutional pipeline, cost formula identity, and crop coverage simultaneously — designed to catch rote memorisers who know MSP exists but conflate CACP's scope and the cost basis of the 2018 commitment.

### FCI, PDS & Buffer Stock

The Food Corporation of India (FCI), established under the FCI Act 1964, performs three core functions: **procurement** of wheat and rice at MSP from farmers, **storage** in Central Pool, and **distribution** to states under the National Food Security Act (NFSA) 2013 at Central Issue Prices (CIP). Under NFSA, priority households receive **5 kg of foodgrains per person per month** at highly subsidised rates (rice ₹3/kg, wheat ₹2/kg, coarse grains ₹1/kg historically; under PM Garib Kalyan Anna Yojana extended through 2028, grains are now provided **free of cost** to ~81.35 crore beneficiaries). FCI operates under two procurement models: **Centralised Procurement** (FCI directly procures or states hand over stocks to FCI, which then stores and distributes) and **Decentralised Procurement System (DCP)** where states procure, store, and distribute themselves using central funds — reducing FCI's logistical burden. **Buffer stock norms** (operational stocks) are set quarterly: as of January 1, the norm is 138 lakh MT (wheat) + 76 lakh MT (rice) = 214 lakh MT; strategic reserves (7.5 lakh MT + 3 lakh MT) are additional. The Shanta Kumar Committee (2015) recommended FCI restructuring, moving DCP to all states, and direct cash transfers instead of in-kind grain support. Under NFSA, **intra-state transportation and handling charges from FCI depots to Fair Price Shops are borne by State Governments**, not the Centre — a critical institutional distinction directly tested in PYQs.

**UPSC Traps:**
- The PYQ (referenced in study material) traps students into thinking the Centre bears intra-state distribution costs from FCI depots to FPS — the Centre bears the food subsidy and central procurement costs, but intra-state handling/transport is a **State Government responsibility**.
- Students confuse "food subsidy burden shared equally between Centre and States" — in reality, the Centre bears the dominant share of food subsidy (MSP + procurement + storage + distribution to state boundary), while states bear only intra-state logistics; it is not an equal 50:50 split.
- The Central Issue Price (CIP) is often mistakenly assumed to still be at ₹3/₹2/₹1 rates — under PMGKAY extended to 2028, the effective CIP is zero (free distribution), making any statement about CIP rates technically outdated for current affairs questions.

**Question format:** Statement-based or direct single-answer format testing the Centre–State financial responsibility split under NFSA, and the distinction between operational buffer norms and strategic reserves — catches students who memorise NFSA entitlements without understanding the institutional cost-sharing architecture.

### e-NAM & APMC Reform

The **National Agriculture Market (e-NAM)** is a pan-India electronic trading portal launched in **April 2016** that networks existing APMC (Agricultural Produce Market Committee) mandis into a unified online market, enabling price discovery through competitive bidding and reducing intermediary layers. It is implemented by the **Small Farmers' Agribusiness Consortium (SFAC)** under the Ministry of Agriculture, with the government providing a one-time grant of **₹75 lakh per mandi** for integration (hardware, software, internet infrastructure). As of 2025, **1,389 mandis across 23 states and 4 UTs** have been integrated. A critical prerequisite for e-NAM integration was that states must amend their APMC Acts to: (i) allow a **single trading licence** valid across the state, (ii) permit **single-point levy of market fee**, and (iii) provide for **electronic auction** as a mode of price discovery — states that did not reform their APMC Acts could not integrate. The 2020 Farm Acts (subsequently repealed in 2021) had attempted to enable trade outside APMC mandis at the national level, but post-repeal, the APMC reform agenda reverted to state discretion. Farmers benefit through transparent price signals, real-time market data, and reduced broker dependence, though logistical challenges (quality assaying, transportation from farm to mandi) remain barriers to full utilisation. **(Not yet directly tested as a UPSC PYQ in 2022–25 in the analysed set.)**

**UPSC Traps:**
- Students confuse e-NAM's implementing agency — it is **SFAC** (a society under MoA), not FCI, NABARD, or NHB; the implementing-agency-swap is a classic UPSC distractor.
- The ₹75 lakh grant is sometimes confused with a loan or a revolving fund — it is a **one-time non-repayable grant per mandi** specifically for IT infrastructure, not a credit facility.
- The APMC reform requirement is often understated: e-NAM does **not** bypass APMC mandis — it digitises them; the misconception that e-NAM allows farmers to sell outside mandis (like the repealed Farm Acts intended) is a frequent conceptual error.

**Question format:** Statement-based or matching format pairing e-NAM features with correct institutional/financial details — designed to catch students who know e-NAM exists but confuse its implementing agency, the financial model, and its relationship to (rather than replacement of) APMC mandis.

### PM-KISAN

Pradhan Mantri Kisan Samman Nidhi (PM-KISAN), launched in **February 2019**, provides direct income support of **₹6,000 per year in three equal instalments of ₹2,000 every four months** directly into the bank accounts of eligible farmer families through DBT. Eligibility covers all **landholding farmer families** (husband, wife, minor children) irrespective of farm size — the scheme was initially restricted to small/marginal farmers (≤2 hectares) but was universalised in **June 2019**. Key **exclusion categories** include: institutional landholders; farmer families with members who are/were constitutional post holders, current/former Ministers/MPs/MLAs/MLCs/Mayors/District Panchayat Chairpersons; serving/retired government employees drawing ≥₹10,000/month pension (excluding Multi-Tasking Staff/Class IV/Group D); professionals (doctors, engineers, lawyers, CAs, architects registered with professional bodies); and those who paid income tax in the last assessment year. As of 2025, approximately **9.3–9.5 crore farmers** receive benefits annually, with total disbursement exceeding ₹3.45 lakh crore cumulatively since inception. The scheme is **100% centrally funded**, requiring no state contribution, distinguishing it from many other agriculture schemes with Centre–State cost-sharing. Aadhaar linkage is mandatory for continued benefit, and the scheme integrates with the PM-KISAN portal for real-time tracking and grievance redressal. **(Not yet directly tested as a UPSC PYQ in 2022–25 in the analysed set.)**

**UPSC Traps:**
- Students frequently believe PM-KISAN remains restricted to small/marginal farmers (≤2 ha) — it was **universalised in June 2019** to cover all farmer families regardless of land size; any statement restricting it to small farmers is incorrect for post-2019 context.
- The exclusion of income taxpayers is often confused as applying to farmers themselves — it applies to any **member of the farmer's family** having paid income tax; this subtle distinction matters in statement-based options.
- PM-KISAN is sometimes mistakenly described as having Centre–State cost-sharing — it is **fully centrally sponsored** (100% Central share), unlike schemes such as PMFBY or crop insurance where states co-contribute the premium subsidy.

**Question format:** Statement-based (often 3 statements) testing: the universalisation timeline, specific exclusion categories, the instalment structure, and funding pattern — designed to catch students who know the headline number (₹6,000/year) but are fuzzy on eligibility boundaries and the funding model.

### PMFBY (Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana)

Launched in **Kharif 2016**, PMFBY replaced the earlier NAIS and MNAIS schemes and operates on an **actuarial premium** model where the government (Centre + State) subsidises the difference between the actuarial premium charged by the insurance company and the **farmer's share of premium** — capped at **2% for Kharif crops, 1.5% for Rabi food/oilseed crops, and 5% for annual commercial/horticultural crops**. The remaining premium (the actuarial amount minus farmer's share) is split between Centre and State in a **50:50 ratio** for general states and **90:10 (Centre:State) for North-Eastern states** to incentivise NE participation. A critical structural change came in **2020**: PMFBY was made **voluntary for farmers** (previously it was compulsory for loanee farmers taking KCC/crop loans) — this reduced enrolled acreage significantly but improved scheme targeting. The scheme covers **pre-sowing to post-harvest losses** including localised calamities (hailstorm, landslide, inundation) and post-harvest losses for up to 14 days; it uses **Crop Cutting Experiments (CCEs)** increasingly supplemented by remote sensing and technology for yield estimation. A companion scheme, **Restructured Weather Based Crop Insurance Scheme (RWBCIS)**, covers weather parameter deviations rather than yield losses, operating with the same premium structure. As of 2024-25, approximately **4 crore farmers** are enrolled, and claims of over ₹1.5 lakh crore have been paid since inception. **(Not yet directly tested as a UPSC PYQ in 2022–25 in the analysed set.)**

**UPSC Traps:**
- The 2020 voluntary shift is a major trap: statements describing PMFBY as **compulsory for loanee farmers** are incorrect for the current framework — it became optional for all farmer categories including those with crop loans, reversing the original design.
- The Centre–State premium subsidy split is frequently misquoted as uniform 50:50 — it is 50:50 for **general states** but **90:10** for NE states; any statement claiming a flat uniform split is false.
- Students confuse the farmer's premium cap for commercial/horticultural crops (5%) with the kharif cap (2%) — the 5% cap is significantly higher and reflects the higher actuarial risk of horticulture; conflating all crop categories under a single premium figure is a common error that UPSC exploits in multi-statement questions.

**Question format:** Statement-based (3–4 statements) testing the premium structure across crop types, the Centre–State subsidy split including NE differentiation, the voluntary/compulsory status post-2020, and coverage scope (pre-sowing to post-harvest) — designed to exploit students who memorise only the "2% and 1.5%" figures without knowing the commercial crop rate, NE exception, or the 2020 policy reversal.

### BOP Accounts

The Balance of Payments (BoP) is a systematic double-entry accounting record of all economic transactions between residents of a country and the rest of the world over a given period. It has three components: the **Current Account** (merchandise trade in goods, trade in services, primary income like investment earnings, and secondary income/transfers like remittances); the **Capital Account** (capital transfers, acquisition/disposal of non-produced, non-financial assets); and the **Financial Account** (FDI, FPI, external borrowings, forex reserve changes). In India's structure, the current account shows a persistent **merchandise trade deficit** that is partially offset by a services surplus (India's IT, BPO, professional services) and large remittance inflows — remittances reached USD 118.7 billion in 2023-24 (over 10% of India's $1 trillion gross current account inflows in FY25) making India the world's top remittance recipient, ahead of Mexico ($68 billion). Net foreign capital inflows under the financial account stood at a modest $18 billion during April–September 2025. The 2024 PYQ tested whether trade flows (e.g., apple imports from the USA) reflect BOP current account dynamics, where relative prices, exchange rates, and domestic production capacity determine actual merchandise composition.

**UPSC Traps:**
- Confusing the **Capital Account** with the **Financial Account** — UPSC exploits the fact that most candidates treat them as one; in IMF's BPM6 framework (adopted by India/RBI), the Financial Account (FDI, FPI, reserves) is distinct from the Capital Account (capital transfers).
- Assuming a **current account surplus** means overall BoP surplus — India can have a current account deficit yet a positive overall BoP if capital/financial account inflows (FDI + FPI + ECBs) more than compensate, which is typically the case.
- Treating **remittances as part of the Capital Account** — they are secondary income under the Current Account (transfer payments), not capital flows.

**Question format:** Statement-based (2–3 statements, "which is/are correct") or assertion–reason; designed to catch candidates who conflate financial account with capital account, or misplace remittances within BoP sub-components.

### Current Account Deficit

India's Current Account Deficit (CAD) = Merchandise Trade Deficit − Net Invisibles Surplus (services + primary income + secondary income/transfers). India structurally runs a **merchandise trade deficit** (imports >> exports in goods, particularly crude oil, gold, electronics) but partially offsets it via a **services surplus** (net service receipts rose to USD 44.5 billion in Q2 FY25 from USD 39.9 billion in Q2 FY24) and massive remittance inflows. India's CAD/GDP has ranged from near-zero to about **−3.8% of GDP** during stress periods (e.g., FY23 due to commodity price spikes); in Q2 FY25 it moderated significantly. As of Q2 FY26, India's CAB as % of GDP stands at approximately **−1.3%**, compared to large surpluses in export-oriented economies (Singapore: +17.7%, Vietnam, Germany) and deficits in Brazil (−3.8%) and the UK. A CAD is financed via the financial account (FDI, FPI, ECBs) — a rising trade deficit can depreciate the rupee if not offset by capital inflows. The top 5 remittance source countries for India (FY2023-24) are USA (27.7%), UAE, UK, Saudi Arabia, and Singapore; top recipient states are Maharashtra (20.5%), Kerala, and Tamil Nadu.

**UPSC Traps:**
- Stating that **increase in trade deficit leads to appreciation of rupee** — the correct answer (from MCQ analysis) is **depreciation**, as greater demand for foreign exchange to pay for imports weakens the domestic currency; UPSC tests whether candidates invert cause and effect.
- Treating **invisibles** as only remittances — the invisibles account includes services trade (IT exports, tourism), primary income (investment returns), and secondary income (remittances); conflating these leads to underestimating India's true current account offset capacity.
- Assuming CAD is always harmful — a moderate CAD financed by stable FDI (productive capital inflows) signals healthy investment demand; UPSC tests nuanced understanding vs. the reflex that CAD = crisis.

**Question format:** Data-interpretation or statement-based; tests whether candidates know what offsets India's merchandise deficit and the directional relationship between trade deficit magnitude and currency pressure.

### NEER & REER

**NEER (Nominal Effective Exchange Rate)** is a weighted average of bilateral nominal exchange rates of the rupee against a basket of currencies of major trading partners, with weights based on trade shares — it is **not adjusted for inflation differentials**. **REER (Real Effective Exchange Rate)** adjusts NEER for relative price levels (inflation differential between India and trading partners): REER = NEER × (Domestic Price Index / Foreign Price Index). An **increase in NEER = rupee appreciation** in nominal terms against the trading partner basket (correct as per the 2022 PYQ). An **increase in REER above 100** indicates the rupee is **overvalued** relative to its base year, meaning Indian exports become less price-competitive — it does NOT indicate improved competitiveness (this was the trap in Statement 2 of the 2022 question). Critically, when **domestic inflation exceeds foreign inflation**, REER rises faster than NEER — the gap between them widens — because the real exchange rate deteriorates even if the nominal rate holds steady; this is the core relationship tested in Statement 3 of the 2022 PYQ (confirmed correct). RBI publishes both NEER and REER using a 36-currency trade-weighted basket; REER is a key metric for assessing India's export competitiveness and currency misalignment.

**UPSC Traps:**
- **Statement 2 trap (2022 PYQ):** Claiming that an increase in REER indicates *improvement* in India's competitiveness — the opposite is true; higher REER = overvaluation = costlier Indian exports = weaker external competitiveness. UPSC directly exploited this inversion.
- Confusing **direction of NEER movement with rupee depreciation** — an increase in the NEER index means rupee has *appreciated* (you need fewer rupees per unit of foreign currency basket), but students conditioned to think "higher number = weaker rupee" get this wrong.
- Treating **NEER and REER as equivalent** when inflation differentials are small — when India's inflation persistently exceeds that of trading partners (as was typical 2011–2014), REER diverges significantly upward from NEER even with a stable nominal rate, affecting competitiveness assessment.

**Question format:** Multi-statement format (3 statements, "which are correct"); designed to catch partial knowledge — candidates often get Statement 1 right and Statement 3 wrong, or accept the false Statement 2 because it sounds intuitive that a "higher real rate = stronger economy."

### IMF Facilities & SDR

The IMF provides lending through multiple facilities differentiated by purpose, income status of borrower, and conditionality level. **Standard facilities** include the Stand-By Arrangement (SBA — short-term BOP support for all members) and Extended Fund Facility (EFF — medium-term structural adjustment). **Emergency/rapid facilities** — directly tested in the 2022 PYQ — include: **Rapid Financing Instrument (RFI)**: available to **all IMF member countries** for urgent BOP needs with minimal conditionality (used extensively during COVID-19); and **Rapid Credit Facility (RCF)**: available exclusively to **low-income countries (LICs)**, providing zero-interest emergency financing. Both RFI and RCF are distinct from ADB, World Bank, or UN programs — they are exclusively IMF instruments, which was the precise factual discrimination the 2022 question tested. **Special Drawing Rights (SDR)** are an IMF-created international reserve asset allocated to members in proportion to their quotas — SDR is **not a currency** but an accounting/reserve unit whose value is based on a basket of five currencies (USD, EUR, CNY, JPY, GBP); India's IMF quota is approximately **2.75%** (post-2023 quota review), giving it a corresponding voting share and SDR allocation. India has never accessed IMF emergency credit in recent decades but holds SDR allocations as part of its foreign exchange reserves.

**UPSC Traps:**
- **ADB trap (Wrong Option A in 2022 PYQ):** ADB offers emergency financing (e.g., COVID-19 Active Response and Expenditure Support — CARES), but the specific named instruments RFI and RCF belong only to the IMF; candidates familiar with ADB emergency programs incorrectly attribute these to ADB.
- **World Bank trap (Wrong Option D in 2022 PYQ):** The World Bank has analogous rapid instruments (e.g., Catastrophe Deferred Drawdown Options), but RFI and RCF are IMF-specific; the institutional confusion between IMF (monetary/BOP support) and World Bank (development lending) is a recurring UPSC trap.
- **SDR as currency misconception:** SDRs are frequently described as "IMF currency" in popular writing — UPSC exploits this by testing whether candidates know SDRs cannot be used directly in private market transactions and are not legal tender; they are reserve assets allocated between central banks and the IMF.

**Question format:** Direct single-answer ("related to provisions of lending by which organisation") — deceptively simple format that requires precise institutional knowledge; wrong options all name real international financial institutions with superficially similar functions, testing exact attribution over general familiarity.

## Financial Markets

# Financial Markets — UPSC Revision Material

---

### FDI vs FPI

Foreign Direct Investment (FPI) involves equity investment of **10% or more** in a company with managerial intent, while Foreign Portfolio Investment (FPI) involves equity stakes **below 10%** primarily for financial returns without control. The critical RBI rule is "Once an FDI, always an FDI" — if an existing FDI stake falls below 10%, it is not reclassified as FPI and need not be restored to 10% or above. Entry routes are: **Automatic Route** (no prior RBI/Government approval needed; covers ~90% of FDI inflows; includes agriculture, telecom, airports, insurance) and **Government Approval Route** (requires Ministry clearance plus compulsory DPIIT concurrence where additional conditions are stipulated). Regardless of route, **CCEA approval is mandatory if FDI value exceeds ₹5,000 crore**. FPI investors require a **SEBI licence/registration** to invest in Indian markets, whereas FDI under the Government Approval Route requires specific Ministry/Department approval. The 2025 Sabka Bima Sabki Raksha Act enabled **100% FDI in insurance**, previously capped at 74%. UPSC tested the sectoral cap angle in the 2022 e-commerce question — specifically the restriction that foreign entities cannot own more than **25% of seller inventory** on their own marketplace platforms.

**UPSC Traps:**
- From the 2022 e-commerce PYQ: candidates assume FDI sectoral caps do not apply to digital/e-commerce firms because they are "marketplace" models — but DPIIT Press Notes explicitly restrict inventory ownership thresholds.
- From the 2024 PYQ analysis on FPI licensing: candidates conflate the FDI automatic route (no approval) with FPI (which still requires SEBI registration) — both involve foreign currency flows but have entirely different regulatory gatekeepers.
- Trap of assuming "once FDI falls below 10% it becomes FPI" — the RBI rule explicitly preserves FDI classification regardless of subsequent stake dilution.

**Question format:** Statement-based true/false pairs testing threshold knowledge (10% rule, CCEA threshold, inventory cap); UPSC exploits the instinct to treat all foreign investment uniformly regardless of size or intent.

---

### Masala Bonds and Green/Transition Bonds

Masala Bonds are **rupee-denominated bonds issued by Indian entities to overseas buyers** to raise funds from foreign markets — the critical feature is that the **currency risk is borne by the foreign investor**, not the Indian issuer, because repayment is in rupees. The name was coined by the **International Finance Corporation (IFC), the investment arm of the World Bank**, to reflect Indian culture. Though the bond is denominated in rupees (e.g., a ₹100 bond paper is issued), the Indian company receives the **dollar equivalent** at prevailing exchange rates — the foreign investor accepts rupee exposure. Masala Bonds are a subset of **External Commercial Borrowings (ECB)** and can be issued to raise foreign capital without taking on forex risk domestically. **Sovereign Green Bonds (SGrBs)** are issued by the **Government of India** (first issued in FY2022-23) to fund green infrastructure projects — proceeds are ring-fenced for environmentally sustainable expenditure. **Transition Bonds** (a SEBI-defined subcategory of green debt securities) are specifically designed for **hard-to-abate sectors** like steel and cement to fund gradual decarbonisation — not immediate green transformation. The distinction between Masala Bonds (corporate, currency risk on investor) and SGrBs (sovereign, rupee-denominated, domestic currency risk) is a live UPSC trap.

**UPSC Traps:**
- Candidates assume that because Masala Bonds are issued "abroad," the Indian company bears foreign exchange risk — the reverse is true; the rupee denomination transfers forex risk to the overseas investor.
- Conflating Sovereign Green Bonds (issued by GoI for green projects) with Masala Bonds (issued by Indian corporates for general ECB purposes) — the issuer identity and use-of-proceeds ring-fencing are entirely different.
- Assuming Transition Bonds are the same as Green Bonds — SEBI distinguishes them specifically for sectors where immediate decarbonisation is not feasible (steel, cement), not for already-clean projects.

**Question format:** Definition-recall and statement-correctness format; UPSC exploits currency-risk attribution confusion and issuer-identity conflation between sovereign and corporate bond categories.

---

### Government Securities

Government Securities (G-Secs) are **sovereign debt instruments issued by the RBI on behalf of the Central Government** and carry **zero default risk** due to sovereign guarantee. **Treasury Bills (T-Bills)** are **short-term, zero-coupon instruments** issued at a discount and redeemed at face value — available in three tenors: **91-day, 182-day, and 364-day**. Because T-Bills are zero-coupon, there is no periodic interest payment; the investor's return is entirely the **discount at issuance**. **Dated G-Secs** are **long-term instruments (1 year to 40 years)** that carry a fixed or floating **coupon rate** paid semi-annually. **State Development Loans (SDLs)** are G-Secs issued by **State Governments** through the RBI, analogous to dated securities but carrying a marginally higher yield than Central Government securities due to slightly higher perceived risk. **Inflation-Indexed Bonds (IIBs)** are a variant where both **principal and coupon payments are adjusted against CPI inflation**, protecting investors from real return erosion — they offer lower nominal yields but guaranteed real returns. The 2022 UPSC question on IIBs tested whether candidates understand that inflation protection in IIBs comes from **principal/coupon indexation to CPI**, not from equity conversion or market-linked returns.

**UPSC Traps:**
- From the 2022 Convertible Bonds PYQ: candidates conflate "conversion to equity provides inflation indexation" — but equity provides inflation hedge only indirectly through asset appreciation, not through contractual CPI linkage as in IIBs.
- Assuming T-Bills pay periodic coupons like dated G-Secs — T-Bills are strictly zero-coupon, discount-based instruments with no interim cash flows.
- Treating SDLs as Central Government instruments — they are issued by State Governments and carry a yield spread over central G-Secs, making them slightly riskier despite also being government-backed.

**Question format:** Statement-based pairs and "which of the following is/are correct" format; UPSC exploits confusion between zero-coupon T-Bills and coupon-bearing dated G-Secs, and between inflation protection mechanisms across instrument types.

---

### Money Market vs Capital Market

The **money market** deals in **short-term debt instruments with maturity less than one year** — instruments include Treasury Bills, Commercial Paper (CP), Certificates of Deposit (CDs), Call Money, and CBLO/TREPS. The **capital market** deals in **long-term instruments** — equities, corporate debentures, dated G-Secs, and long-term bonds — with no fixed upper maturity. **Bonds** are capital market instruments that provide **fixed coupon returns** contractually predetermined at issuance, unlike **equities** which offer variable dividends dependent on company performance. **Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs)** are **financial instruments** traded on exchanges (capital market instruments), representing a basket of securities. A **currency swap** is a **derivative financial instrument** in which two parties exchange principal and interest payments in different currencies — it is a financial instrument regardless of whether it trades on an exchange. The 2024 UPSC question explicitly tested the boundary between financial instruments (ETFs, currency swaps) and **physical/real assets** (motor vehicles) — motor vehicles are tangible goods, not financial claims, and cannot be classified as financial instruments under any standard definition.

**UPSC Traps:**
- From the 2024 PYQ Option C/D: candidates include motor vehicles as financial instruments by confusing "asset" (generic) with "financial asset" (a claim on future cash flows or ownership rights) — the confusion arises because vehicles are listed alongside financial products in a multiple-choice format designed to exploit category errors.
- From the 2024 PYQ on who can trade bonds: candidates assume retail investors have the same access to corporate bonds and G-Secs as institutional investors — retail access to **corporate bonds** remains restricted in India, though RBI's Retail Direct Scheme (2021) opened **G-Sec** access directly.
- Placing ETFs in the money market because they are "liquid" — ETFs are exchange-traded capital market instruments; liquidity does not determine market classification, maturity does.

**Question format:** "Which of the following is/are financial instruments" and statement-pair format; UPSC deliberately mixes physical assets with financial instruments and institutional vs retail access to exploit definitional imprecision.

---

### CBLO and TREPS

**CBLO (Collateral Borrowing and Lending Obligation)** was a **secured money market instrument** operated through the **Clearing Corporation of India Ltd (CCIL)** in which borrowers pledged **Government Securities as collateral** to obtain short-term funds, typically overnight to 90-day maturity. The fundamental feature was **tri-party security**: the G-Sec collateral was held by CCIL as the central counterparty, reducing counterparty risk significantly compared to unsecured call money. In **2018, CBLO was replaced by TREPS (Tri-Party Repo)** — the mechanism is functionally similar (G-Secs as collateral, CCIL as tri-party agent) but TREPS is formally structured as a **repo agreement** (sale with agreement to repurchase), giving it a cleaner legal framework aligned with global standards. Both CBLO and TREPS operate in the **money market** (sub-one-year maturity) and are used primarily by **mutual funds, banks, insurance companies, and NBFCs** for short-term liquidity management. The key distinction from **call money** is that CBLO/TREPS is **secured** (collateral-backed) whereas call money is **unsecured** interbank lending. UPSC tested CBLO in 2024 as a definition-recall question, focusing on the collateral mechanism and the transition to TREPS as the defining fact candidates must retain.

**UPSC Traps:**
- Confusing CBLO/TREPS with **call money** — both are overnight/short-term money market instruments, but call money is unsecured interbank lending while CBLO/TREPS requires G-Sec collateral; the security structure is the defining distinction.
- Assuming CBLO is still operational — it was **discontinued and replaced by TREPS in 2018**; questions framing CBLO in present tense are testing whether candidates know about this transition.
- Treating TREPS as a capital market instrument because it involves G-Secs — the instrument's maturity (sub-one-year, typically overnight) places it firmly in the money market regardless of the collateral type.

**Question format:** Direct definition-recall and "what is X" single-answer format (as seen in 2024); UPSC exploits the tendency to confuse secured vs unsecured money market segments and to be unaware of the 2018 CBLO→TREPS transition.

## Fiscal Policy & Budget

# FISCAL POLICY & BUDGET — UPSC Revision Material

---

### Deficit Types

Revenue Deficit = Revenue Expenditure − Revenue Receipts; it signals the government is borrowing to fund **current consumption** (salaries, interest payments, subsidies) — spending that creates no future returns. Fiscal Deficit = Total Expenditure − Total Receipts **excluding borrowings**; it is the total borrowing requirement of the government from all sources. Primary Deficit = Fiscal Deficit − Interest Payments; it isolates the current fiscal imbalance from the debt-service burden inherited from past borrowing — a zero primary deficit means current borrowing only services old debt. Effective Revenue Deficit (introduced via FRBM Amendment 2012) = Revenue Deficit − Grants given to states for creation of capital assets; it strips out revenue expenditure that actually creates capital at the state level, giving a cleaner picture of truly "wasteful" borrowing. Budget 2026-27 estimates: Fiscal Deficit 4.3% of GDP (down from 4.4% in 2025-26), Revenue Deficit 1.5% of GDP, Primary Deficit 0.7% of GDP. UPSC tests the precise arithmetic relationship between these — specifically whether a given combination of numbers **allows inference** of the fiscal position (the 2025 numerical PYQs on ₹80,000 cr revenue expenditure and ₹50,000 cr fiscal deficit test exactly this: you **cannot** determine revenue deficit without knowing revenue receipts, and fiscal deficit minus non-debt capital receipts = borrowing requirement).

**UPSC Traps:**
- Assuming revenue deficit can be calculated from revenue expenditure alone — the 2025 PYQ tests that the answer **cannot be derived** without knowing revenue receipts, exploiting the misconception that expenditure figures alone define the deficit
- Treating non-debt capital receipts (disinvestment proceeds) as eliminating the fiscal deficit — they only reduce the **borrowing requirement**; the remaining gap is still financed through debt, so fiscal deficit remains as stated
- Conflating primary deficit with fiscal deficit — interest payments are excluded from primary deficit but are a **real liability** already incurred; confusing this causes miscalculation of what "zero primary deficit" means

**Question format:** Numerical/inferential statement-based (2025 style) — gives partial data (only expenditure OR only partial receipts) and asks what **can be inferred**; designed to catch students who apply the formula without checking for missing variables. Also appears as "arrange in ascending order of magnitude" or "which includes/excludes interest payments."

---

### Receipts Classification

All government receipts split into **Revenue Receipts** (do not create liability or reduce assets — taxes, dividends, interest received, fees, grants from foreign governments) and **Capital Receipts** (create liability OR reduce assets — borrowings, disinvestment, loan repayments received). The defining test: does the receipt obligate the government to repay or does it diminish something already owned? Borrowings create liability (must be repaid with interest) → Capital Receipt. Disinvestment reduces government's equity stake in PSUs (reduces assets) → Capital Receipt. Interest **received** by government on loans it has given out is **Revenue Receipt** — it does not create a liability; it is a return on a prior capital outflow. Spectrum auction proceeds are **Revenue Receipts** because spectrum is not permanently alienated — it is leased for 20–30 years, not sold. Dividends/profits from PSUs and RBI surplus transfer = Non-Tax Revenue Receipts (revenue account). Household financial savings flow into government borrowing instruments (G-secs, T-bills), making households indirect financiers of the fiscal deficit; dated securities issued at market-related rates are the **primary component** of India's internal debt.

**UPSC Traps:**
- The 2025 PYQ trap: claiming "interest received creates a government liability" — interest received is money coming **in** on loans already given; it is a Revenue Receipt with zero liability creation (Statement III was wrong; options A, C, D exploited this misconception)
- Classifying disinvestment as a Revenue Receipt because it involves "income" from selling shares — it reduces government assets (equity in PSU), making it a Capital Receipt, not a tax or fee
- Assuming household savings do **not** finance government debt — the 2025 PYQ confirms both that household savings flow to government borrowings AND that dated securities at market rates dominate internal debt; both statements were correct

**Question format:** Multi-statement TRUE/FALSE (2025 style, 3 statements, choose correct combination); specifically crafted so that two statements are obviously correct and one contains a subtle directional error (liability vs. return confusion). The "which of the following are Capital Receipts" list format also appears, planting grants and spectrum proceeds as decoys.

---

### Expenditure Classification

Revenue Expenditure covers all spending that neither creates assets nor reduces liabilities: salaries, pensions, interest payments, subsidies, maintenance grants — it is recurring and consumed within the year. Capital Expenditure creates physical/financial assets (roads, buildings, defence equipment) or reduces liabilities (loan repayments by government) — it has a lasting balance-sheet effect. The key asymmetry: when government **lends money**, the principal disbursed is Capital Expenditure (creates a financial asset — the loan receivable), but the **interest received back** is Revenue Receipt; when government **borrows**, the principal received is Capital Receipt (creates liability), and the **interest paid** is Revenue Expenditure. Grants given by the central government to states appear in the **Revenue account** (Revenue Expenditure) even when the states use those grants to build capital assets — this creates the Effective Revenue Deficit correction. In 2026-27, capital expenditure is projected to grow ~11.5% over revised estimates, signalling continued fiscal push on infrastructure. Plan vs. Non-Plan distinction was abolished post-2017 (following Rangarajan Committee); the current classification is simply Capital and Revenue.

**UPSC Traps:**
- Classifying loan principal repayment **by government** as Revenue Expenditure because "paying off debt feels like an expense" — it reduces a liability, making it Capital Expenditure
- Assuming grants to states are Capital Expenditure because states use them to build assets — grants appear on the **Revenue** side of the central budget regardless of end-use; only the Effective Revenue Deficit formula adjusts for this
- Confusing "capital expenditure = plan expenditure" after the 2017 merger — Plan/Non-Plan is defunct; Capital/Revenue is the operative classification (not yet directly tested as UPSC PYQ in this exact form, but foundational for deficit arithmetic questions)

**Question format:** Statement-based pairs asking whether a given item is Capital or Revenue expenditure/receipt; UPSC plants items like "interest payment on government borrowings" (Revenue Expenditure) alongside "repayment of loan principal" (Capital Expenditure) to test whether candidates know the asset/liability criterion rather than just memorising lists.

---

### FRBM Act

The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act, 2003 mandates the central government to pursue fiscal consolidation through legally binding targets and transparency documents. Original targets (FY 2008-09): Fiscal Deficit ≤ 3% of GDP, Revenue Deficit = 0%; these were missed due to 2008-09 global financial crisis and MNREGA-era stimulus, leading to amendments in 2008, 2012, and 2015. The 2018 amendment (following N.K. Singh Committee) introduced a **fiscal deficit target of 3% of GDP** with an **Escape Clause** allowing deviation of up to **0.5 percentage points** under: national security/calamity, far-reaching structural reforms, agricultural collapse, or extraordinary decline in output. The 2012 amendment introduced **Effective Revenue Deficit** as a new metric and targeted its elimination. FRBM requires tabling of three statements with the Budget: Medium Term Fiscal Policy Statement (3-year rolling targets), Fiscal Policy Strategy Statement (justification for deviations), and Macroeconomic Framework Statement. The N.K. Singh Committee also recommended targeting a **debt-to-GDP ratio of 60%** (40% Centre + 20% States) as the nominal anchor; Budget 2026-27 targets 50±1% debt-to-GDP by 2030, with current estimate at 55.6% of GDP.

**UPSC Traps:**
- Confusing the escape clause quantum — it is **0.5 percentage points** deviation, not 1% or "at the Finance Minister's discretion"
- Treating FRBM as purely a fiscal deficit rule — it is equally a **Revenue Deficit** elimination statute; the 2012 amendment adding Effective Revenue Deficit and the ongoing 1.5% revenue deficit show the revenue side remains unmet
- Conflating FRBM debt target with the 16th Finance Commission framework — the 50±1% by 2030 is a Budget announcement; the N.K. Singh Committee's 60% (centre+state combined) is the FRBM-embedded benchmark (not yet directly tested as a standalone UPSC PYQ concept, but appears embedded in Finance Commission and deficit questions)

**Question format:** Statement-based — "which documents must be tabled under FRBM" or "which conditions activate the escape clause"; also appears as matching (committee → recommendation) format. The cognitive trap is conflating N.K. Singh Committee recommendations with the original 2003 Act provisions.

---

### Finance Commission

The Finance Commission is a **quasi-judicial constitutional body** established under Article 280; it is constituted every five years to recommend: (i) vertical devolution — share of central taxes to be given to states collectively (currently **41%** as recommended by the 15th FC and retained by the 16th FC for FY2026-27); (ii) horizontal devolution — how to distribute that 41% among states using a weighted formula; (iii) grants-in-aid to states under Article 275. The 15th Finance Commission (covering 2021-26) used **five criteria** for horizontal devolution: Population (2011 Census, 15%), Area (15%), Income Distance (45%), Forest & Ecology (10%), Tax & Fiscal Effort (2.5%), Demographic Performance (12.5%) — a departure from the 14th FC's formula; crucially it added **Demographic Performance** to reward states with lower fertility rates. For FY2022-26, the 15th FC recommended **₹4,800 crore in grants for educational outcomes**. The 16th Finance Commission has been constituted (Chairman: Arvind Panagariya) and Budget 2026-27 provides ₹1.4 lakh crore to states as Finance Commission Grants (including Rural/Urban Local Body and Disaster Management Grants). Finance Commission grants are **constitutionally mandated** (charged to Consolidated Fund) whereas NITI Aayog makes policy recommendations with no fiscal transfer authority.

**UPSC Traps:**
- Miscounting the horizontal devolution criteria of the 15th FC — the 2023 PYQ tested exactly how many criteria were used **beyond** the three named (population, area, income distance); the answer depends on knowing the full five-criteria formula; options exploit undercounting (picking 2 instead of the correct 2 additional ones: Forest/Ecology + Tax effort + Demographic Performance = actually **3** criteria beyond the three named, making 6 total weighted components — students confuse number of criteria vs. number of weightages)
- Treating NITI Aayog grants as equivalent to Finance Commission grants — NITI Aayog has **no constitutional mandate** for fiscal transfers; only Finance Commission grants are charged to the Consolidated Fund
- Confusing the 14th FC's 42% devolution with the 15th FC's 41% — the reduction from 42% to 41% was to account for newly formed UTs of J&K and Ladakh (whose share was carved out); both figures appear in UPSC traps

**Question format:** "How many of the following criteria" (how-many format, 2023 PYQ) and multi-statement TRUE/FALSE (2025 PYQ on ₹4,800 crore grants and devolution share). The how-many format is high-difficulty because it requires exact enumeration of formula components, not just conceptual recognition.

---

### Black Money & Benami Transactions

**Black Money (Undisclosed Foreign Income and Assets) Act, 2015** targets **foreign** undisclosed assets/income held by Indian residents abroad; penalty = **tax + 300% penalty** on undisclosed amount; non-filing or concealment = imprisonment of 3–10 years; the investigating agency is the **Income Tax Department** (under the Finance Ministry). **Benami Transactions (Prohibition) Amendment Act, 2016** (amending the 1988 Act) targets **domestic** benami property — property held in the name of a fictitious person or a person who has not paid for it, while the beneficial owner remains hidden; the enforcing agency is the **Initiating Officer under the Income Tax Department**, and attachment/confiscation is handled by the **Adjudicating Authority** with appeal to the Appellate Tribunal; punishment = 1–7 years imprisonment + fine up to 25% of fair market value. PMLA (Prevention of Money Laundering Act, 2002) is enforced by the **Enforcement Directorate (ED)** under the Finance Ministry, which handles laundering of proceeds of scheduled offences — distinct from Benami (IT Department) and Black Money Act (IT Department). The government's primary fiscal concern with black money, per UPSC's 2025 framing, is **diversion into unproductive assets** — real estate, gold, jewellery, precious items — which reduces productive capital formation and distorts asset prices, rather than the politically salient but secondary concern of political donations or recession causation.

**UPSC Traps:**
- The 2025 PYQ trap: selecting "political funding" or "recession causation" as the government's **primary** worry regarding black money — UPSC signals the answer is the economic distortion angle (unproductive asset diversion, real estate inflation, reduction of tax base and productive investment)
- Confusing which agency enforces each law: ED enforces PMLA; **Income Tax Department** enforces both the Black Money Act 2015 and the Benami Act 2016 — UPSC plants ED as the Benami enforcer because ED is more publicly prominent in high-profile cases
- Treating the Black Money Act 2015 and Benami Act as covering the same territory — Black Money Act covers **foreign** undisclosed assets; Benami covers **domestic** property held in another's name; the distinction in jurisdiction (overseas vs. domestic) is the precise line UPSC exploits

**Question format:** Single-best-answer (2025 style — "which of the following effects has been the **main** cause of worry") requiring prioritisation among multiple valid-sounding options; also appears as match-the-column

### Production Linked Incentive (PLI) Scheme

Launched in 2020 under the 'Aatmanirbhar Bharat' vision, the PLI Scheme incentivises companies to achieve incremental sales **above a defined base year threshold**, with the government paying a fixed percentage incentive on those incremental sales — it is NOT a subsidy on total production or capital expenditure by default. The scheme now spans **14 key sectors** including mobile phones, pharmaceuticals, specialty steel, white goods, food processing, and advanced chemistry cells, with a total outlay of **₹1.97 lakh crore**. PLI for Specialty Steel (PLI 1.2) has entered a third round, specifically targeting import reduction in Defence, Space, and Power sectors through end-to-end domestic manufacturing. Both domestic companies and foreign companies operating in India are eligible, making it a hybrid instrument — neither purely import-substitution nor purely export-promotion. India's ~3.2% share of global goods exports is a structural, historical figure predating PLI (2020), meaning PLI cannot causally explain that share even though PLI beneficiaries do exist. Budget 2025 continues allocations across all 14 sectors with phased disbursement linked to verified incremental production data.

**UPSC Traps:**
- **[2023 PYQ Trap — Option A]** The most tested trap is assuming Statement-II (PLI benefiting firms) *explains* Statement-I (India's 3.2% export share) — UPSC explicitly designed the question so both statements are individually true but causally unrelated; choosing "II explains I" is the intended error
- PLI is sometimes confused with a capital subsidy or input subsidy — it is strictly an **output/sales-linked** incentive on *incremental* turnover above the base year, not total turnover
- Confusing the number of sectors: PLI covers **14 sectors**, not 10 or 12 — wrong figures for sector count are a standard distractor in statement-based questions

**Question format:** Assertion–Reason (Statement-I / Statement-II) format as in 2023; tests **concept-relationship** skill — the cognitive error targeted is conflating correlation of two true facts with causation.

---

### Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC)

The IBC (2016) established a **unified, creditor-in-control** framework replacing a fragmented regime of multiple overlapping statutes (SICA, SARFAESI partial overlap, Companies Act provisions). The adjudicating authority is the **National Company Law Tribunal (NCLT)**; appeals go to **NCLAT (National Company Law Appellate Tribunal)**; the prescribed timeline is **180 days + 90-day extension = 270 days maximum**, with an outer cap of **330 days** including litigation time. On resolution, proceeds follow a strict **waterfall mechanism**: Insolvency Resolution Process Costs → Secured Financial Creditors → Unsecured Financial Creditors → Operational Creditors → Equity Shareholders. The RBI's 2024 report confirmed IBC accounted for **48% of total bank recoveries in FY 2023–24**, making it the dominant recovery channel over SARFAESI and DRT. NCLT's institutional design, based on the 1999 Eradi Committee recommendations, is considered structurally outdated for current caseload; pre-packaged insolvency for **MSMEs** (introduced 2021) allows faster resolution with lower legal costs. Economic Survey 2024-25 specifically flags the need for improved operational efficiency and time reduction, especially for MSMEs where legal costs are disproportionate.

**UPSC Traps:**
- Confusing **NCLT** (first instance, adjudicating authority) with **NCLAT** (appellate) — statement-based questions routinely swap these roles
- The timeline trap: stating the IBC timeline as **180 days** ignoring the 90-day extension and the 330-day outer cap including litigation — all three numbers are separately testable
- Assuming operational creditors rank above unsecured financial creditors in the waterfall — they do not; operational creditors rank **below** unsecured financial creditors

**Question format:** Statement-based (how-many-are-correct) format; tests **threshold knowledge** of procedural specifics — the cognitive error is partial recall of the timeline or waterfall sequence.

---

### MUDRA Loans (Pradhan Mantri MUDRA Yojana)

MUDRA (Micro Units Development and Refinance Agency) provides loans to non-corporate, non-farm micro and small enterprises through a **three-tier (now four-tier)** structure: **Shishu** (up to ₹50,000), **Kishore** (₹50,001–₹5 lakh), **Tarun** (₹5 lakh–₹10 lakh), and **Tarun Plus** (₹10 lakh–₹20 lakh — introduced in **Budget 2024**). MUDRA is not a direct lending bank; it **refinances** Member Lending Institutions (MLIs) — commercial banks, RRBs, MFIs, NBFCs — which in turn lend to end borrowers; MUDRA does not directly disburse to beneficiaries. Loans under MUDRA are classified as priority sector lending and do not require collateral below the Tarun ceiling, reducing access barriers for first-generation entrepreneurs. The **RuPay MUDRA Card** is issued to borrowers for working capital withdrawals, enabling digital transactions. Tarun Plus was specifically created for entrepreneurs who have successfully repaid prior MUDRA loans and seek to scale up, recognising a graduation pathway within the scheme. No processing fee is charged for Shishu category loans.

**UPSC Traps:**
- Treating MUDRA as a **direct lending institution** — it is a refinancing/regulatory body; loans are disbursed by MLIs, not MUDRA directly
- Ignoring the **Tarun Plus** tier (₹10L–₹20L, Budget 2024) and treating Tarun (₹10L) as the ceiling — any statement calling ₹10 lakh the maximum MUDRA loan limit is now incorrect
- Confusing MUDRA with **Stand-Up India** (which specifically targets SC/ST and women entrepreneurs for ₹10L–₹1 crore greenfield loans) — both involve bank loans to small entrepreneurs but have entirely different eligibility and purpose

**Question format:** Statement-based or direct factual; most likely tests **tier limits and the nature of MUDRA as a refinancing agency** — the cognitive error targeted is confusing the institutional role or using pre-2024 tier structure.

---

### PMJDY and Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT)

Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (PMJDY, launched August 2014) is a financial inclusion scheme providing **zero-balance savings accounts** with a **RuPay debit card** and built-in **₹1 lakh accidental insurance cover** (enhanced to ₹2 lakh for new account holders post-August 2018). The **overdraft facility** of up to **₹10,000** is available to account holders with satisfactory conduct (six months), with at least **₹2,000 usable without conditions** — the 2018 revision doubled the overdraft from ₹5,000 to ₹10,000 and extended eligibility to all adults (earlier only one per household). PMJDY accounts form the **foundational layer of the JAM Trinity** (Jan Dhan–Aadhaar–Mobile), which is the backbone infrastructure for Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) — enabling government subsidies, pensions, scholarships, and MGNREGA wages to be credited directly, eliminating intermediaries. Over **53 crore accounts** have been opened under PMJDY (as of recent data), of which a majority are in rural/semi-urban areas and ~55% are women's accounts. DBT has been extended to over 300 central government schemes, with cumulative transfers exceeding ₹38 lakh crore, yielding estimated savings through leakage reduction verified by PAHAL (LPG DBT), PM-KISAN, and scholarship schemes.

**UPSC Traps:**
- Confusing the overdraft limit: **₹10,000** is current ceiling, **₹2,000** is the no-conditions sub-limit — stating ₹5,000 reflects the pre-2018 limit and is a deliberate distractor
- Stating that PMJDY requires a **minimum balance** — it is explicitly a zero-balance account; any statement implying minimum balance requirements is false
- Conflating PMJDY with **Pradhan Mantri Jeevan Jyoti Bima Yojana (PMJJBY)** or **Pradhan Mantri Suraksha Bima Yojana (PMSBY)** — these are separate insurance schemes often bundled with Jan Dhan accounts but are not part of PMJDY itself

**Question format:** Statement-based (true/false combination); tests **threshold knowledge** of specific financial parameters — the cognitive error is using outdated figures (₹5,000 overdraft) or conflating bundled insurance schemes with PMJDY's own features.

### Social Security Schemes: PM-SYM, APY, PM Surakshit Matritva Abhiyan

**PM-SYM (Pradhan Mantri Shram Yogi Maan-dhan):** A voluntary, contributory pension scheme for **unorganised sector workers** (street vendors, construction workers, domestic workers, etc.) with a monthly income below ₹15,000. Entry age is **18 to 40 years** (NOT 21–40, which is the most tested trap). At age 60, beneficiaries receive a **minimum guaranteed pension of ₹3,000/month**; contributions are **age-specific** (younger entrants pay less, e.g., ₹55/month at 18; older entrants pay more, e.g., ₹200/month at 40), with the central government making an **equal matching contribution**. A **family pension** of 50% of the beneficiary's pension is available to the spouse; beneficiaries cannot be covered under NPS, ESIC, or EPFO. **APY (Atal Pension Yojana):** Targets workers in the unorganised sector aged **18–40**, with a guaranteed pension of ₹1,000–₹5,000/month at age 60 depending on contribution amount; from October 2022, income taxpayers are ineligible to enrol. **PM Surakshit Matritva Abhiyan:** Provides **free antenatal care (ANC)** to pregnant women on the **9th of every month** at government health facilities and designated private facilities; focuses on the second and third trimesters and ensures at minimum one quality ANC check-up. It does not guarantee care at *any* government facility on any day — care is structured around the fixed monthly date. Private sector obstetricians/gynaecologists and radiologists voluntarily participate through the scheme.

**UPSC Traps:**
- **[2024 PYQ Trap — PM-SYM Statement 1]** Entry age stated as **21–40** is explicitly false — the correct entry age is **18–40**; this was the direct distractor in the 2024 question
- **[2024 PYQ Trap — PM Surakshit Matritva Abhiyan]** Stating the scheme guarantees ANC at "any government health facility" at any time is false — it is specifically structured around the **9th of each month** at designated facilities; the universality claim is the trap
- Confusing PM-SYM with **APY**: both target 18–40 age group in unorganised sector, but APY offers ₹1,000–₹5,000 pension (variable by contribution), while PM-SYM offers a **flat ₹3,000/month minimum** guaranteed pension regardless of contribution variation; also, income taxpayers are barred from APY (2022) but no such bar exists for PM-SYM eligibility (already barred by income threshold and EPFO/ESIC exclusion)

**Question format:** Statement-based (evaluate Statement 1 and Statement 2 independently); tests **threshold knowledge** of precise eligibility parameters and operational details — the cognitive error is approximate recall (21 vs 18 for entry age) or over-generalising service delivery conditions.

## Inflation & GDP

# INFLATION & GDP — UPSC Revision Material

---

### CPI vs WPI (cpi_wpi)

CPI (Consumer Price Index) measures **retail-level price changes** experienced by final consumers across goods and services; it is compiled by **MoSPI on a monthly basis** and serves as India's **headline inflation measure** used by the RBI for monetary policy under the Flexible Inflation Targeting (FIT) framework (target: 4% ± 2%, i.e., 2–6% band). WPI (Wholesale Price Index) measures price changes at the **wholesale/producer entry stage** — covering only goods (not services), excluding indirect taxes, making it more sensitive to raw material and input cost changes. The **Office of the Economic Adviser (DPIIT)** releases WPI monthly. A critical structural difference: CPI food weight is being revised downward to **~36.75%** (from 45.86%) in the new 2024-base CPI series, based on the 2023-24 Household Consumption Expenditure Survey (HCES), while WPI assigns weights using **net traded value** methodology. India is transitioning from WPI toward a **Producer Price Index (PPI)** — PPI measures prices at producer level before goods/services reach consumers, excludes indirect taxes, covers both goods and services, and uses **Supply Use Tables (SUTs)** for weights — but PPI does not yet exist as a live index in India (DPIIT is finalising methodology). The 2022 UPSC question directly tested that **RBI** — not the Department of Consumer Affairs, not FSDC — is the statutory body responsible for price stability under the RBI Act, 1934.

**UPSC Traps:**
- **Department of Consumer Affairs as inflation controller** (Opt A, 2022): Exploits the fact that Consumer Affairs handles food supply/PDS, making it seem connected to price control — but it has no monetary policy mandate whatsoever.
- **FSDC as primary inflation body** (Opt C, 2022): FSDC coordinates systemic financial stability across regulators but is not the inflation-targeting authority; confusing financial stability with price stability is the precise error tested.
- **WPI covers services** — a recurring trap: WPI covers only goods; CPI covers goods and services. Students conflate the two because both track "prices in the economy."

**Question format:** Direct single-answer MCQ ("which body is responsible for…") — tests institutional mandate recall; designed to catch those who confuse sectoral ministry functions with monetary authority.

---

### Core vs Headline Inflation (core_headline_inflation)

**Headline inflation** is the total CPI change including all components — food, fuel, and core — and is the number reported as India's official retail inflation (e.g., CPI rose to **3.2% in February 2026**, highest in ten months). **Core inflation** strips out food and fuel/energy prices because these are supply-side volatile components; it captures underlying, demand-driven price pressure and is considered a better signal for monetary policy transmission. The **four major components driving core inflation** (per Economic Survey 2025-26) reflect demand-pull dynamics, whereas food inflation — responsible for **44 basis points of the 47-basis-point increase** in February 2026 inflation — is predominantly cost-push/supply-side. **Base effect** is a statistical phenomenon: when the price level in the base period was unusually high, the current year-on-year inflation rate appears artificially low (and vice versa) — this distorts headline readings without representing real price change. During COVID-19 (2020), crude oil prices fell but the government raised taxes, meaning **WPI declined** (input-sensitive, no tax component) while **CPI remained relatively stable** — demonstrating that the two indices can diverge sharply during supply shocks because of structural differences in composition and tax treatment. Rural inflation exhibits **greater volatility** than urban inflation due to higher food weights in rural consumption baskets, though state-level outcomes have broadly converged within RBI's tolerance band.

**UPSC Traps:**
- **Confusing cost-push (supply-side) food inflation with demand-pull core inflation**: WPI's sensitivity to raw material prices (metals, cotton, crude) makes it a cost-push indicator — students incorrectly assume WPI and CPI move together, which the COVID crude oil example directly disproves.
- **Assuming base effect is a real price change**: Base effect can make inflation appear to fall sharply even when absolute price levels remain high — UPSC tests this as a statement-true/false scenario.
- **WPI capturing services**: WPI does not capture service price changes; CPI does — a trap recycled from the CPI/WPI distinction but frequently reappearing in statement-based questions on "which index better reflects consumer burden."

**Question format:** Statement-based (how-many-correct format); tests ability to distinguish demand-pull vs cost-push, and to identify what each index structurally includes/excludes rather than just knowing their names.

---

### GDP, GNP, NNP, GVA (gdp_gva_national_income)

**GDP (Gross Domestic Product)** measures total value of goods and services produced within India's geographic boundary in a year, regardless of producer nationality. **GNP = GDP + Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA)** — it captures output by Indian nationals/residents globally. **NNP = GNP − Depreciation (Capital Consumption Allowance)** — "Net" always means depreciation has been subtracted; "National" means NFIA is added. **GVA (Gross Value Added) = GDP − Net Product Taxes (i.e., taxes minus subsidies on products)**: India shifted its headline national accounts presentation to **GVA at basic prices** after the 2011-12 base year revision, and the relationship is: **GDP at market price = GVA at basic prices + Product taxes − Product subsidies**. The 2024 UPSC question tested the **fixed capital vs working capital** distinction within national income accounting: **fixed capital** comprises durable assets used repeatedly over multiple production cycles (machinery, buildings, computer, farmer's plough), while **working capital** comprises raw materials and intermediates consumed in a single production cycle (yarn, petrol). A farmer's plough is **fixed capital** (reused across seasons), not working capital — and yarn is **working capital** (consumed in weaving), not fixed capital. India's **CSO (now NSO under MoSPI)** releases national accounts data; the **base year is currently 2011-12**, with revision to 2022-23 under discussion.

**UPSC Traps:**
- **Farmer's plough classified as working capital** (Opt A/D, 2024): Exploits the intuition that a plough is a "basic/low-tech" input used by a poor farmer, leading students to conflate simplicity of tool with working capital nature — but durability/reusability defines fixed capital regardless of sophistication.
- **Yarn classified as fixed capital** (Opt C, 2024): Yarn is raw material physically transformed and consumed in production — students see it as a "capital input to the weaver" and incorrectly label it fixed capital, missing the "consumed vs reused" criterion.
- **Confusing GVA and GDP**: Students assume India reports GDP as primary output measure; post-2011-12, India's primary sectoral measure is GVA at basic prices, with GDP derived by adding net product taxes — reversing the addition/subtraction of taxes and subsidies is a persistent error.

**Question format:** Matching pairs (correct/incorrect pairs format, "how many pairs are correctly matched") — tests precise definitional recall with deliberate mislabelling of familiar items; cognitive trap is familiarity bias (plough = farmer = basic input = "working").

---

### Real vs Nominal GDP, GDP Deflator, PPP (real_nominal_gdp)

**Nominal GDP** values output at **current market prices** — it rises with both actual output growth and inflation, so it overstates real economic expansion during inflationary periods. **Real GDP** values output at **constant base-year prices** (currently 2011-12 in India), isolating true volume growth from price effects; India's real GDP growth for 2024-25 is estimated at **~6.4%** (First Advance Estimate). The **GDP Deflator = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) × 100** — it is a **broader inflation measure than CPI** because it covers the entire economy's output (not just a consumer basket), includes investment goods and government expenditure, and is not based on a fixed basket (it updates weights automatically). Unlike CPI, the GDP deflator has **no fixed base-year basket** and covers **export prices** but **excludes import prices** (since imports are not part of domestic production). **PPP (Purchasing Power Parity)** adjusts GDP comparisons across countries by equalising the purchasing power of currencies — at PPP, India is the **3rd largest economy** globally, whereas at market exchange rates (nominal USD), India is approximately **5th**. India's **Advance Estimate (1st) is released by NSO/MoSPI in January** of the fiscal year itself; the **Second Advance Estimate** follows in February, with subsequent revisions. The GDP deflator and CPI can diverge significantly — for example, when food inflation is high (raising CPI) but investment goods prices fall (pulling deflator lower). (Not yet directly tested as a standalone UPSC PYQ in the 2022-2025 window, but embedded in national income accounting questions.)

**UPSC Traps:**
- **GDP deflator = CPI**: Students conflate the two inflation measures; the deflator covers the full production boundary while CPI covers only a fixed consumption basket — and they can move in opposite directions.
- **India is largest economy at market exchange rates**: The PPP vs nominal GDP distinction is a classic trap — India ranks 3rd at PPP but ~5th nominally; conflating these two rankings appears in comparative economy statements.
- **"Net" in NNP means net of taxes** (spillover from GVA concept): "Net" always refers to depreciation subtraction in national accounts terminology, never to taxes — taxes are the "market price vs factor cost/basic price" adjustment, not the gross-to-net adjustment.

**Question format:** Statement-based or direct MCQ — tests whether candidates can distinguish what the deflator covers vs CPI covers, and whether they know India's ranking changes depending on the measurement methodology used; designed to catch those who memorise rankings without understanding the measurement basis.

## Monetary Policy & Banking

# MONETARY POLICY & BANKING — UPSC Dense Revision Material

---

### Repo Rate, Reverse Repo, MSF, Bank Rate

The **repo rate** is the rate at which RBI lends overnight funds to scheduled commercial banks against government securities as collateral; it is decided by the **Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)** and is binding on RBI — MPC has no authority over CRR, SLR, or reverse repo rate directly. The **rate corridor** structure: SDF rate (floor) → Reverse Repo → Repo Rate → MSF Rate (ceiling), where MSF = repo rate + 0.25% and Bank Rate is aligned with MSF rate. The **Marginal Standing Facility (MSF)** allows banks to borrow up to 2% of their SLR holdings from RBI at a penal rate, providing a safety valve against unanticipated liquidity shocks — critically, banks access MSF *at their discretion*, whereas repo facility is available *at RBI's discretion*. The **SDF (Standing Deposit Facility)**, introduced in April 2022, replaces the fixed reverse repo as the floor of the corridor — banks park excess funds with RBI under SDF without needing to offer government securities as collateral (uncollateralised). The **Bank Rate**, published under Section 49 of the RBI Act 1934, is the rate at which RBI rediscounts commercial bills — operationally it equals the MSF rate and is used as a penalty rate for non-maintenance of CRR/SLR. The **operating target** of monetary policy is the Weighted Average Call Rate (WACR), which RBI anchors around the repo rate through day-to-day liquidity management.

**UPSC Traps:**
- [2023 PYQ] Trap: Assuming post-pandemic central bank rate hikes contradict standard monetary theory — in fact both statement I (rate hikes occurred) and statement II (higher rates reduce aggregate demand) are correct and causally linked; the trap exploits the false assumption that one of the two must be wrong.
- MPC decides only the repo rate; candidates confuse MPC's mandate with full authority over all RBI policy rates including CRR/SLR — MPC has zero authority over these instruments.
- MSF and SDF are at *bank's* discretion; repo/reverse repo are at *RBI's* discretion — reversing this direction is a classic trap in statement-based questions.

**Question format:** Statement-pair (Statement I + Statement II with causal relationship) — designed to catch candidates who know one fact but misread the logical relationship between the two; also appears as direct assertion-reason format.

---

### CRR and SLR

**Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR)** is the average daily balance that scheduled banks must maintain with RBI in cash as a percentage of their Net Demand and Time Liabilities (NDTL); it is maintained under the **RBI Act, 1934** and RBI can change it without any statutory floor or ceiling. **Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR)** is maintained under the **Banking Regulation Act, 1949** and requires banks to hold a specified proportion of NDTL in cash, gold, or government-approved securities — banks hold SLR with *themselves*, not with RBI. Current levels (as of 2025): CRR ≈ **4%**, SLR ≈ **18%** — both are treated as bank reserves for calculating the money multiplier. The **money multiplier** (M3/M0) stood at **6.21** as of December 2025 (adjusted MM = 6.0 when SDF is included); the multiplier depends on the currency-deposit ratio (determined by public behaviour) and the reserve-deposit ratio (determined by CRR/SLR regulations). The critical distinction: CRR funds earn **no interest** for banks, while SLR securities (G-secs) earn interest, making CRR a more punitive liquidity tool. Changes in CRR directly impact the monetary base available for credit creation — a 1% hike in CRR with NDTL of ₹200 lakh crore absorbs approximately ₹2 lakh crore from the system.

**UPSC Traps:**
- [2024 NBFC PYQ context] NBFCs are not required to maintain CRR/SLR with RBI — confusing NBFC regulatory requirements with scheduled bank requirements is a recurring trap, particularly when LAF access is bundled in the same question.
- CRR is maintained with RBI in cash; SLR is maintained by banks with themselves — reversing the custodian (treating both as held with RBI) is a common error.
- RBI Act governs CRR; Banking Regulation Act governs SLR — swapping the governing statutes is tested in definition-recall questions.

**Question format:** Statement-based (true/false on regulatory details), sometimes paired with NBFC/LAF access questions; tests precise statutory attribution and operational mechanics simultaneously.

---

### OMO, MSS, and Sterilisation

**Open Market Operations (OMO)** are RBI's purchase or sale of government securities in the open market to inject or absorb liquidity — OMO *purchase* injects money (expansionary), OMO *sale* absorbs money (contractionary). **Sterilisation** specifically refers to OMO used to neutralise the impact of **capital inflows** on domestic money supply: when foreign capital enters India, rupee liquidity rises, so RBI *sells* G-secs via OMO to absorb that excess liquidity — this is the direct UPSC-tested definition. The **Market Stabilisation Scheme (MSS)**, introduced in 2004 after the forex reserve surge, is a separate instrument: RBI issues Treasury Bills/dated securities specifically to absorb excess liquidity from capital inflows, but the proceeds are **sterilised in a separate MSS account** with RBI (not used for government expenditure). OMO and MSS differ crucially: OMO uses existing government securities and the proceeds go to the government's normal account; MSS involves fresh issuance solely for liquidity absorption, with the cash locked away. **Inflation response via OMO**: high inflation → RBI sells G-secs → absorbs liquidity → reduces money supply → dampens demand → controls inflation. **Rupee depreciation response**: RBI sells forex reserves (dollars) to support the rupee — this is distinct from OMO in domestic markets.

**UPSC Traps:**
- [2022 PYQ Statement 1] High inflation → RBI *buys* G-secs (WRONG) — correct action is RBI *sells* G-secs to contract liquidity; this trap exploits confusion between the direction of OMO during inflation vs. recession.
- [2024 Digital Rupee PYQ Statement 2] Rapid rupee depreciation → RBI *buys* government securities (WRONG) — rupee depreciation requires contractionary (not expansionary) OMO; RBI should sell securities or intervene in forex market.
- Sterilisation is confused with settlement oversight (a regulatory/payment function) — [2023 PYQ] wrong option B tested exactly this by presenting clearing/settlement as a liquidity management tool.

**Question format:** Direct single-answer (which activity = sterilisation), and multi-statement sets testing OMO direction across different economic scenarios; the cognitive trap is reversing buy/sell direction under stress conditions.

---

### Bank Types

India's banking structure: **Scheduled Commercial Banks (SCBs)** are listed in the Second Schedule of the RBI Act and must maintain CRR with RBI; they include public sector banks (PSBs), private banks, foreign banks, small finance banks, and payments banks. **Payments Banks** can accept deposits up to ₹2 lakh per customer, cannot lend, must invest 75% of deposits in G-secs/T-bills — they bridge last-mile banking access. **Small Finance Banks (SFBs)** can lend and accept deposits without a ceiling, with 75% of loans required to go to priority sector (PSL) — at least 50% of PSL must be loans below ₹25 lakh. **Cooperative banks** are regulated dually — urban cooperative banks (UCBs) by RBI (for banking) and state governments/Registrar of Cooperative Societies (for cooperative law). **Syndicated lending** involves multiple banks jointly extending a large loan to a single borrower — the fundamental purpose is **risk distribution**, reducing individual bank's exposure. The **Banks Board Bureau (BBB)** is constituted by the government to advise PSBs on strategy, capital plans, board appointments; the **RBI Governor chairs BBB**.

**UPSC Traps:**
- [2022 BBB PYQ] Trap: Assuming RBI Governor does *not* chair BBB — both statements (Governor as Chairman, BBB helping PSBs with strategy) were correct; wrong options excluded Statement 1 or added a false Statement 3.
- [2024 Syndicated Lending PYQ] Syndicated lending is confused with consortium lending or co-lending — the core fact is *risk distribution* among lenders, not merely joint administration of a loan.
- Foreign banks operating in India face **specific RBI prudential regulations** on risk assessment and consortium lending participation — assuming foreign banks operate under identical rules as domestic banks is the tested trap.

**Question format:** Statement-based correctness (which statements about BBB/syndicated lending are correct); tests institutional knowledge of who chairs what and what statutory powers exist.

---

### NBFC

**Non-Banking Financial Companies (NBFCs)** are registered under the Companies Act and regulated by RBI under Chapter III-B of the RBI Act, 1934 — they can lend and make investments but **cannot accept demand deposits** (savings/current accounts) from the public. Critical restriction: NBFCs **cannot access RBI's Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF)** window — LAF is exclusively available to **scheduled banks and primary dealers**. NBFCs can accept time deposits (fixed deposits) only if they are **deposit-taking NBFCs (NBFC-D)** with specific RBI approval, subject to strict caps. **NBFC-MFI (Microfinance Institutions)** lend to low-income borrowers — with 85% of net assets as qualifying assets; the income cap for borrowers is ₹3 lakh (rural) — now income-agnostic under revised 2022 RBI framework with loan limit ₹1.25 lakh (general) and ₹2 lakh (for existing members). **Shadow banking** refers to the credit intermediation occurring through NBFCs outside traditional banking regulation — RBI's scale-based regulation (SBR) classifies NBFCs into Base, Middle, Upper, and Top layers with increasing regulatory intensity. **Foreign Institutional Investors (FIIs)** — now termed FPIs — *can* hold Indian government securities subject to RBI-stipulated limits (currently 6% of outstanding stock of G-secs).

**UPSC Traps:**
- [2024 PYQ Statement 1] NBFCs *can* access RBI's LAF window (WRONG) — this is the most directly tested fact; NBFCs are explicitly excluded, only scheduled banks and primary dealers have LAF access.
- [2024 PYQ] Confusing FII eligibility for G-secs — FIIs/FPIs *are* permitted to hold government securities; wrong options bundled the correct FII statement with the false NBFC-LAF claim, trapping candidates who got one right and assumed the other must also be wrong.
- NBFCs are confused with scheduled banks regarding deposit insurance (DICGC coverage) — DICGC covers only bank deposits, not NBFC deposits.

**Question format:** Multi-statement (true/false), frequently pairing one obviously checkable fact with one obscure regulatory restriction; designed to catch candidates who partially know NBFC rules but conflate them with bank rules.

### Digital Payments

**RTGS (Real Time Gross Settlement)**: settlement is **instantaneous and individual** (gross = each transaction settled separately); minimum transaction limit ₹2 lakh; operating hours **9:00 AM – 4:30 PM** on working days (not 24/7); primarily for large-value transactions. **NEFT (National Electronic Funds Transfer)**: settlement is in **batches** (hourly, now 48 batches available 24×7 including weekends and holidays since December 2019); no minimum or maximum transaction limit; suitable for retail transactions. **IMPS (Immediate Payment Service)**: 24×7, real-time, operated by NPCI, limit ₹5 lakh — functions even on holidays. **UPI (Unified Payments Interface)**: real-time, mobile-based, interoperable across banks, operated by NPCI; per-transaction limit ₹1 lakh (₹2 lakh for certain categories like capital markets, IPO). UPI **international merchant payments** are currently accepted in: UAE, France, Germany, Singapore, Bhutan, Nepal, Mauritius, Sri Lanka — from the PYQ set, UAE, France, Germany, and Singapore = **4 countries** (Bangladesh is NOT in the formal international merchant UPI acceptance network). **Digital Rupee (e₹/CBDC)**: issued by RBI, is a **sovereign currency** — legal tender, liability of RBI, not of commercial banks; available in wholesale (e₹-W) and retail (e₹-R) forms.

**UPSC Traps:**
- [2025 RTGS/NEFT PYQ] Trap: RTGS charges on inward transactions differently from NEFT — both have similar charge structures for inward; the trap exploits superficial knowledge of charges while the actual distinction is settlement timing (real-time vs. batch).
- [2025 UPI international PYQ] Bangladesh included in UPI international merchant acceptance (WRONG) — candidates over-count to 5 countries, confusing bilateral UPI remittance corridors (where Bangladesh exists for person-to-person) with formal merchant payment acceptance.
- [2024 Digital Rupee PYQ] Digital rupee is issued by commercial banks, not RBI (WRONG) — e₹ is a direct RBI liability, equivalent to physical currency, distinct from commercial bank money (deposits).

**Question format:** How-many format (count correct countries/statements), statement-based with precise operational details; tests threshold knowledge (exact hours, exact countries, exact settlement mechanics) rather than conceptual understanding.

## Taxation

# TAXATION — UPSC Revision Material

### GST Structure

GST, introduced via the 101st Constitutional Amendment (2016) and operational from July 1, 2017, replaced a fragmented indirect tax system with a unified destination-based consumption tax — revenue accrues to the state where goods/services are finally consumed, not where produced. **Intrastate transactions** attract both CGST (Central Goods and Services Tax) and SGST (State GST) simultaneously, while **interstate transactions** attract IGST (Integrated GST), collected entirely by the Centre and then apportioned — IGST eliminates the earlier inter-state CST and enables seamless input tax credit (ITC) across state lines. Union Territories without legislatures levy UTGST instead of SGST. The **GST Council** is constituted under **Article 279A** of the Constitution; its voting mechanism requires a **three-fourths weighted majority** — Centre's vote carries one-third weight, all states' votes together carry two-thirds weight, making Centre-state consensus structurally mandatory. The **Compensation Cess** was levied on luxury/sin goods to compensate states for revenue loss for five years post-GST implementation (ending June 2022, extended due to COVID borrowings); the cess on items like tobacco, SUVs, aerated drinks continues under a modified framework. The **GST Appellate Tribunal (GSTAT)** is established under **Section 109 of the CGST Act, 2017**, with 50 benches proposed across India, phased starting with state capitals; UP hosts the maximum number of benches. GST 2.0 reforms include rate rationalisation (proposed two-slab simplification), inclusion of petroleum and alcohol, enhanced IT compliance, and support for MSMEs. Online gaming, casinos, and horse racing attract **28% GST on the full face value of bets** (not just platform earnings), following a contentious legal dispute resolved in 2023. *(not yet directly tested as a standalone UPSC PYQ in the mapped set)*

**UPSC Traps:**
- **IGST is shared only with states, not collected by the state of origin** — candidates conflate IGST as a state-collected tax; it is collected by the Centre and then distributed, unlike SGST which goes directly to the transacting state.
- **GST Council voting is NOT simple majority and NOT equal Centre-state weight** — the weighted 1/3 (Centre) vs 2/3 (states collectively) split means no single state can block a decision, but the Centre alone cannot pass one either; confusing this with a simple majority or a unanimous requirement is a frequent error.
- **Article 279A establishes the GST Council — not Article 280** (which establishes the Finance Commission); the two are routinely swapped in statement-based questions.

**Question format:** Predominantly **statement-based (2–3 statements, how-many-correct)** testing constitutional article numbers, voting weight, and CGST/SGST/IGST applicability distinctions — designed to catch candidates who know GST broadly but miss structural specifics like the Article number or the exact voting fraction.

---

### Direct vs Indirect Tax

A **direct tax** is one where the statutory incidence (legal liability) and economic impact (actual burden) fall on the **same person** — it cannot be shifted forward to a third party; examples include Income Tax, Corporate Tax, and Capital Gains Tax. An **indirect tax** is shiftable — the legal liability rests on the seller/producer, but the economic burden is passed forward to the consumer through higher prices; GST, customs duty, and excise duty are indirect taxes. **Progressive taxation** means the marginal rate rises with income, making the tax burden proportionally higher on the wealthy — India's personal income tax follows a progressive slab structure (currently six slabs under the new regime: 0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, 30%). **Regressive taxes** impose a relatively higher burden on lower-income groups as a proportion of income — GST on essential goods can be regressive if not mitigated by exemptions (zero-rated items). India's **direct-to-indirect tax ratio** has been improving: direct taxes now contribute over 55% of total tax revenue, indicating a more progressive tax system compared to the pre-reform era when indirect taxes dominated. The **individual taxpayer base** has expanded from 3.26 crore (AY2013-14) to nearly **7.26 crore (AY2024-25)** — more than doubling — driven by digital filing systems, pre-filled returns, the Annual Information Statement (AIS), and formalisation via GST and UPI. Corporate tax collections more than doubled between FY21 and FY25 (aggregate corporate profits rose from ₹2.5 trillion to ₹7.1 trillion). The declining **cost of collection** alongside an expanding taxpayer base signals structural administrative efficiency gains. *(not yet directly tested as UPSC PYQ in the mapped set)*

**UPSC Traps:**
- **"Direct tax = paid directly to government" is NOT the defining criterion** — the defining criterion is non-shiftability of burden; Securities Transaction Tax (STT) is paid directly to the government but is classified as a direct tax precisely because of burden incidence, not the payment mechanism.
- **Corporate tax is direct; GST paid by a company is indirect** — a common confusion arises when candidates see a company paying both; the categorisation depends on who ultimately bears the economic burden, not who writes the cheque.
- **Progressive ≠ Direct, and Regressive ≠ Indirect** — these are separate analytical dimensions; a specific indirect tax (e.g., GST on luxury goods at 28%) can be progressive in effect, while a flat-rate direct tax (e.g., a poll tax) would be regressive; UPSC exploits this by pairing these concepts incorrectly in statement form.

**Question format:** **Pairs/matching or statement-based** questions asking which taxes fall into which category, or which feature (shiftability, progressivity) belongs to which type — designed to exploit the conflation of the payment mechanism with the incidence criterion.

---

### Tax Buoyancy

**Tax buoyancy** measures the responsiveness of tax revenue to changes in national income: it is defined as the **percentage change in tax revenue divided by the percentage change in GDP**. A buoyancy coefficient **greater than 1** means tax revenues grow faster than the economy — indicating that the tax system is capturing economic growth efficiently and that the tax base is expanding or compliance is improving. A coefficient **less than 1** implies tax revenues lag GDP growth, suggesting structural inefficiencies, evasion, or a narrow tax base. India's gross tax revenue rose from an average of **10.8% of GDP (FY16–FY20)** to approximately **11.5% of GDP (FY22–FY25)**, reflecting improved buoyancy in the post-pandemic period driven by GST stabilisation, direct tax reforms, and digitalisation. Personal income tax collections grew **6.8% YoY** and corporate tax collections grew **7.8% YoY** as of November 2025 (against Budget Estimates). **Tax buoyancy is different from tax elasticity**: elasticity measures revenue response to tax rate/base changes with policy held constant, while buoyancy captures the combined effect of both policy changes and administrative improvements — UPSC has tested this distinction implicitly. The taxpayer base expansion (from ~3.26 crore to ~7.26 crore individuals) is a structural driver of sustained buoyancy; the pandemic caused a temporary spike (FY20–21) due to COVID-related fiscal measures, after which the trajectory corrected but then improved structurally due to formalisation. Revenue receipts increased from ~8.5% of GDP (pre-pandemic average) to ~9.1% of GDP (post-pandemic), underscoring sustained buoyancy across both direct and indirect tax heads. *(not yet directly tested as UPSC PYQ in the mapped set)*

**UPSC Traps:**
- **Tax buoyancy > 1 does NOT necessarily mean tax rates were increased** — it can reflect better compliance, base broadening, or faster GDP growth in taxable sectors; confusing buoyancy (a combined measure) with the effect of rate changes is the primary conceptual trap.
- **Tax buoyancy ≠ Tax elasticity** — buoyancy includes the effect of discretionary policy changes (rate hikes, new taxes), while elasticity isolates the automatic revenue response holding policy constant; statements conflating the two are a likely UPSC trap in the economics optional and GS-III.
- **A declining cost of tax collection does NOT imply falling tax revenue** — it implies administrative efficiency (more revenue per unit of collection effort); candidates misread this as a negative fiscal indicator.

**Question format:** **Direct definition + application questions** or **statement-based** asking whether a given scenario (e.g., "GDP grew 7%, tax revenue grew 9%") implies buoyancy > 1 or < 1, or distinguishing buoyancy from elasticity — targets the cognitive error of conflating the formula's numerator/denominator or misattributing buoyancy solely to rate changes.
