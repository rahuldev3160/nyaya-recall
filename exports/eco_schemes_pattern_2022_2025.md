### Overall Trend (2022–2025)

UPSC's economics section has undergone a decisive shift from pure definitional recall toward **applied analytical reasoning**, with questions increasingly requiring candidates to evaluate statements against real-world policy logic — for instance, knowing not just what OMO is but whether buying or selling government securities controls inflation. The question count has risen sharply from 13 in 2023 to 25 in 2025, signalling that economics is becoming a **high-weightage differentiator** in Prelims. Between 2022 and 2024, external sector questions dominated with 19 total, often blending trade policy with constitutional and technology topics — reflecting UPSC's tendency to cross-pollinate economics with polity and science-tech. From 2024 onward, a new precision-testing trend is visible: questions on **specific eligibility thresholds** (PM-SYM entry age, pension amounts), **exact operational mechanics** (RTGS vs NEFT charging rules), and **numerical distinctions** (how many countries accept UPI merchant payments) that punish vague preparation. Government schemes have moved from "what is the scheme" to "what are the specific provisions and exclusions," demanding granular reading of scheme guidelines rather than headline summaries.

### 1. Monetary Policy & Banking

**Questions tested (2022–2025):**
- RBI operations: whether RBI buys or sells government securities under high inflation vs rupee depreciation vs falling US/EU interest rates (2022)
- Banks Board Bureau — chairman identity (Governor of RBI is NOT chairman; it is a separate authority), PSB strategy function (2022)

- NBFC access to LAF window — NBFCs cannot access RBI's LAF (2024)
- FII holdings of Government Securities (2024)
- RBI income sources — buying/selling govt bonds, foreign currency, NOT pension management or currency printing as income (2025)
- Capital flight and External Commercial Borrowing interest cost (2025)

- UPI international merchant payments — number of countries (UAE, France, Singapore = 3 excluding India; Bangladesh and Germany incorrect) (2025)

**What UPSC is testing:** UPSC is testing **operational precision** — not what instruments exist, but *how* they work directionally. The RBI operations question (2022) requires knowing that to fight inflation RBI *sells* securities (contracts money supply), not buys them. The NBFC/LAF question tests a specific **regulatory boundary**: NBFCs, despite being financial entities, cannot access the Liquidity Adjustment Facility — a bank-exclusive privilege. The UPI question tests **current affairs numerical threshold knowledge**: exactly which countries and how many.

**Question format pattern:** Predominantly statement-based (3 statements, which are correct), with the trap being **directional reversal** — e.g., students confuse buying vs selling of securities and their monetary impact. The RTGS/NEFT question used three statements where Statement II (inward charges) was false and Statement III (operating hours restriction) was partially true but reversed, making Option A (I only) a tempting wrong answer.

**Current affairs linkage (2025–2026):**
- RBI transferred ₹2,69,000 crore surplus to the Central Government in 2024–25 — the highest ever — creating a direct question on RBI income sources and dividend transfer mechanisms
- Digital rupee (e₹) expansion: CBDC pilots extended; distinction between wholesale (e₹-W) and retail (e₹-R) versions is a live trigger
- New RBI eligibility norms for Urban Cooperative Banks (UCBs) announced in 2026 — capital adequacy, governance triggers
- UPI acceptance in France and UAE operational since 2024; Bhutan, Mauritius, Nepal also live — specific country count questions likely

**Prediction for 2026:**
1. Statement-based question on RBI's CBDC (digital rupee) — testing whether it is legal tender, bears interest, and can be converted to bank deposits
2. Direct question on which entities can access the LAF window — distinguishing scheduled commercial banks, cooperative banks, NBFCs, and payment banks
3. Question on RBI's OMO vs MSS: which instrument sterilises capital inflows by issuing government securities vs which absorbs liquidity via outright bond purchase

### 1. Repo Rate vs Reverse Repo Rate vs Bank Rate vs MSF

The **Repo Rate** is the interest rate at which the Reserve Bank of India lends short-term funds to commercial banks against government securities as collateral, typically for overnight duration under the Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF). As of June 2025, the repo rate stands at 6.25% (reduced from 6.5% after RBI cut rates in February and April 2025 amid easing inflation). The **Reverse Repo Rate** is the rate at which RBI *borrows* money from commercial banks (i.e., banks park excess funds with RBI) — it is lower than the repo rate and currently stands at 3.35%, though it has not been the operational rate since the introduction of the Standing Deposit Facility (SDF) at 6.00%. The **Bank Rate** (currently 6.75%) is the rate at which RBI lends to commercial banks *without* any security/collateral and for longer tenures — it is automatically pegged 25 basis points above the repo rate and is used for penal purposes. The **Marginal Standing Facility (MSF)** allows scheduled commercial banks to borrow overnight from RBI at a rate 25 basis points *above* the repo rate (currently 6.50%), providing an emergency liquidity window — banks can use their SLR holdings as collateral even beyond the minimum required. UPSC tests this by asking whether students know the *direction* of each rate (who pays whom), the *collateral requirement*, and the *purpose* of each instrument.

**UPSC Traps:**
- **Trap 1:** Students think the Reverse Repo Rate is what banks charge each other — it is specifically what RBI pays banks when they park money with RBI overnight
- **Trap 2:** Confusing Bank Rate with repo rate — Bank Rate has no collateral requirement and is not used for daily liquidity management; repo rate is the operational policy rate
- **Trap 3:** Thinking MSF is only for NBFCs or only for crisis situations — it is available to all scheduled commercial banks daily, and the rate corridor is: SDF (floor) → Repo (policy) → MSF (ceiling)

### 2. CRR vs SLR — Purpose, Current Levels, Who Decides

**Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR)** is the percentage of a bank's net demand and time liabilities (NDTL) that must be maintained as cash deposits with the Reserve Bank of India — banks earn *no interest* on CRR deposits. As of 2025, CRR stands at **4%** (reduced from 4.5% in December 2024 to inject liquidity). **Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR)** is the percentage of NDTL that banks must hold in the form of liquid assets — specifically, gold, government securities (G-Secs), or other RBI-approved securities — and currently stands at **18%**. The critical difference is that CRR is held as idle cash with RBI (no return), while SLR assets are held by the bank itself and *do earn returns* (G-Secs pay coupons). Both ratios are set by the **Reserve Bank of India** under the RBI Act 1934 and the Banking Regulation Act 1949 respectively — they are not set by Parliament or the government directly, though the RBI Act specifies the permissible range within which RBI can operate. Increasing CRR/SLR reduces money available for lending (contractionary); decreasing them is expansionary. The key distinction for UPSC is that SLR assets can be used as collateral for MSF borrowing (banks can temporarily fall below SLR to access emergency liquidity from RBI), whereas CRR cannot be deployed at all.

**UPSC Traps:**
- **Trap 1:** Banks earn interest on SLR holdings (true — they hold G-Secs) but earn NO interest on CRR (a common source of confusion — students think both are sterile reserves)
- **Trap 2:** CRR and SLR are set by the government — FALSE; both are set by RBI within statutory limits defined in the respective Acts
- **Trap 3:** SLR can only be in cash — FALSE; SLR includes gold, government securities, and other approved liquid assets, making it more flexible than CRR

### 3. Open Market Operations (OMO) vs Market Stabilisation Scheme (MSS)

**Open Market Operations (OMO)** are transactions conducted by the RBI in the open market to regulate liquidity in the banking system: when RBI *buys* government securities from banks, it injects rupee liquidity into the system (expansionary); when it *sells* securities, it absorbs liquidity (contractionary). OMOs are the primary tool for **sterilisation of capital inflows** — when large foreign capital enters India, it inflates domestic money supply, so RBI sells securities to absorb the excess rupees. The 2023 UPSC question specifically tested this: "which RBI activity is considered sterilisation?" — the answer is OMO (specifically, selling government securities). The **Market Stabilisation Scheme (MSS)** was introduced in 2004 specifically to deal with the sterilisation problem at scale — RBI issues government securities (Treasury Bills and dated securities) *on behalf of the government* to absorb liquidity from large capital inflows. The critical difference: OMO uses securities *already held by RBI* from its own portfolio, while MSS creates *new securities* specifically for absorption purposes, and the proceeds are held in a separate MSS cash account with RBI (not used for government expenditure). Both achieve liquidity absorption but through different mechanisms.

**UPSC Traps:**
- **Trap 1:** Students think OMO buying is contractionary — it is expansionary (RBI pays money to buy back securities, releasing cash into the system)
- **Trap 2:** MSS securities are permanent government debt — FALSE; they are temporary sterilisation instruments; when conditions normalise, RBI cancels/redeems them, releasing liquidity
- **Trap 3:** Sterilisation hurts the government's fiscal position — MSS securities do not add to fiscal deficit as proceeds go into a separate non-spendable account

---

### 4. Types of Banks — Scheduled, Commercial, Cooperative, Payment, Small Finance

Indian banking comprises five principal categories. **Scheduled Commercial Banks (SCBs)** are those included in the Second Schedule of the RBI Act 1934 — they must have paid-up capital of ₹5 lakh and satisfy RBI's stability criteria; they are eligible for LAF, MSF, and refinancing from RBI. **Cooperative Banks** operate under dual regulation — the cooperative societies law of respective states for registration/management, and RBI for banking functions; they serve agricultural and rural credit needs and include State Cooperative Banks (StCBs), District Central Cooperative Banks (DCCBs), and Primary Agricultural Credit Societies (PACS). **Payment Banks** (introduced 2015) can accept deposits up to ₹2 lakh per customer, offer remittance services, and issue debit cards — but **cannot lend** (no loans or credit cards) and cannot accept NRI deposits; they are full-fledged scheduled banks but with restricted activities. **Small Finance Banks (SFBs)** must extend 75% of their loans to priority sector (agriculture, MSME, low-income groups) and have a minimum 25% branches in unbanked rural areas; they CAN offer both deposits and loans (unlike payment banks). UPSC tests these distinctions extensively, particularly the restrictions on payment banks and the dual-regulatory status of cooperative banks.

**UPSC Traps:**
- **Trap 1:** Payment banks can issue credit cards — FALSE; they can only issue debit cards and prepaid instruments; no lending is permitted
- **Trap 2:** All cooperative banks are regulated only by state governments — FALSE; RBI regulates them for banking functions; NABARD supervises urban cooperative banks' inspection
- **Trap 3:** Small Finance Banks and Payment Banks are the same type of differentiated bank — they are different; SFBs lend to priority sectors while payment banks cannot lend at all

---

### 5. NBFC — Definition, Regulatory Differences from Banks, Shadow Banking Concern

A **Non-Banking Financial Company (NBFC)** is a company registered under the Companies Act 2013 that is engaged in financial activity as its *principal business* — lending, investment, leasing, hire-purchase, insurance, chit funds — but does NOT hold a banking licence. The RBI defines the "principal business" test: if a company's financial assets exceed 50% of its total assets AND income from financial assets exceeds 50% of gross income, it is an NBFC. Key differences from banks: NBFCs **cannot accept demand deposits** (savings/current accounts); they are **not part of the payment and settlement system** (cannot issue cheques); they do **not have deposit insurance** under DICGC; and crucially, as tested in 2024 UPSC, NBFCs **cannot access the RBI's LAF window** — only scheduled commercial banks can. The **shadow banking concern** arises because NBFCs provide credit like banks but face lighter regulation — the collapse of IL&FS (2018) and the DHFL crisis highlighted systemic risks when large NBFCs fail. Systemically Important NBFCs (those with assets ≥ ₹500 crore) face enhanced RBI regulation including leverage norms and disclosure requirements.

**UPSC Traps:**
- **Trap 1:** NBFCs can access RBI's emergency liquidity — FALSE; only scheduled commercial banks access LAF/MSF; NBFCs must rely on market borrowing
- **Trap 2:** Chit funds are NBFCs regulated by RBI — FALSE; chit funds are regulated by state governments under the Chit Funds Act 1982, not RBI
- **Trap 3:** All NBFCs require RBI registration — partially true; only those accepting deposits or above ₹10 crore asset size require mandatory registration; but the "principal business" test applies universally for classification

---

### 6. Sterilisation of Capital Flows

When large capital inflows enter India (foreign investment, NRI remittances, ECB), the foreign currency is sold to RBI in exchange for Indian rupees. This increases the **domestic money supply** (rupees in circulation), which if unchecked causes inflation and appreciates the nominal exchange rate, hurting export competitiveness. **Sterilisation** is the process by which RBI neutralises this increase in money supply — it does so by absorbing the excess rupees through selling government securities (OMO) or issuing new securities under MSS. Sterilisation preserves the exchange rate (prevents sharp rupee appreciation) while preventing inflationary money supply expansion. However, sterilisation has a **fiscal cost**: RBI pays interest on the G-Secs it sells while the foreign reserves it holds earn lower returns (US treasuries yield less than domestic G-Sec coupon rates) — this is the "quasi-fiscal cost of sterilisation." UPSC's 2023 question directly tested this: "which RBI activity constitutes sterilisation?" — the correct answer being Open Market Operations (specifically the sale of government securities to absorb liquidity from capital inflows). Incomplete sterilisation is also a policy choice — allowing some rupee appreciation to reduce import costs and inflation.

**UPSC Traps:**
- **Trap 1:** Sterilisation means preventing capital inflows — FALSE; capital is allowed to flow in, but the *monetary impact* (rupee expansion) is neutralised
- **Trap 2:** RBI buying dollars for reserves is itself sterilisation — FALSE; buying dollars *creates* the liquidity problem (releases rupees); the subsequent OMO sale of G-Secs is the sterilisation step
- **Trap 3:** Sterilisation always fully offsets capital inflows — policy makers often choose partial sterilisation to allow some appreciation as an anti-inflation tool (imported goods become cheaper)

---

### 7. Revenue Deficit vs Fiscal Deficit vs Primary Deficit vs Effective Revenue Deficit

These four deficit concepts form a hierarchy that UPSC tests repeatedly with specific classification scenarios. **Revenue Deficit** = Revenue Expenditure − Revenue Receipts: this represents the government borrowing to meet *day-to-day running costs* (salaries, interest, pensions) that yield no future assets — a chronic revenue deficit is fiscally unsustainable. **Fiscal Deficit** = Total Expenditure − Total Receipts *excluding borrowings*: this measures the government's total financing requirement from borrowings; it is the broadest measure of deficit. **Primary Deficit** = Fiscal Deficit − Net Interest Payments: this strips out the debt-servicing burden to show what new borrowing is needed for *non-interest* expenditure — a zero primary deficit means the government borrows only to service past debt. **Effective Revenue Deficit (ERD)** = Revenue Deficit − Grants for Creation of Capital Assets: introduced by the FRBM Amendment 2012, ERD removes grants that states use for capex from the revenue deficit, giving a more accurate picture of "wasteful" current spending. For Budget 2026–27: Fiscal Deficit = 4.3% of GDP; Revenue Deficit = 1.5% of GDP; Primary Deficit = 0.7% of GDP. Interest payments = fiscal deficit − primary deficit = 3.6% of GDP, accounting for 26% of total expenditure.

**UPSC Traps:**
- **Trap 1:** Fiscal deficit includes borrowings in total receipts — FALSE; fiscal deficit is calculated *excluding* borrowings from receipts (borrowings are what you're measuring the need for)
- **Trap 2:** Zero primary deficit means no new borrowing — FALSE; zero primary deficit means government borrows *only* to pay interest on past debt; it still borrows, just for interest payment
- **Trap 3:** Revenue deficit is always smaller than fiscal deficit — generally TRUE, but students must know WHY (fiscal deficit includes both revenue and capital gaps); a revenue surplus combined with capital deficit is possible (rare)

---

### 8. Capital Receipts vs Revenue Receipts — Classification of Borrowings, Disinvestment, Dividends

**Revenue Receipts** are recurring receipts that do not create any liability or reduce government assets — they include tax revenues (income tax, GST, corporate tax) and non-tax revenues (dividends from PSUs, fees, fines, interest received on loans given by government to states/PSUs). **Capital Receipts** create a liability or cause a reduction in assets — they include: (a) borrowings (market loans, external loans — create repayment liability), (b) recoveries of loans (reduce government's financial assets), and (c) disinvestment proceeds (reduce government's equity holdings = reduction in assets). The 2025 UPSC question directly tested this: "capital receipts create liability OR reduce assets" (Statement I — correct); "borrowings and disinvestment are capital receipts" (Statement II — correct); "interest received on loans creates a liability" (Statement III — WRONG; interest received is revenue receipt, not a liability). The correct answer was B (only II correct as a statement, but the question asked which combination — answer B in that specific framing). Dividends received from PSUs = revenue receipt (not capital), while disinvestment of PSU equity = capital receipt.

**UPSC Traps:**
- **Trap 1:** Interest received on loans by government is a capital receipt — FALSE; it is a revenue receipt (recurring income from assets); the principal recovery is capital receipt
- **Trap 2:** Disinvestment is a revenue receipt because the government "earns" money from it — FALSE; disinvestment reduces government's asset (equity holding), making it a capital receipt
- **Trap 3:** All non-tax revenue is revenue receipt — generally true, but students must distinguish between interest *received* (revenue) and principal *recovered* (capital)

---

### 9. Capital Expenditure vs Revenue Expenditure

**Capital Expenditure (CapEx)** is spending that either creates a new asset or reduces a liability — construction of roads, bridges, railways, purchase of equipment, acquisition of land, loans given to states/PSUs, and repayment of past government debt. CapEx generates future returns and has a longer productive life. **Revenue Expenditure (RevEx)** covers all spending that neither creates assets nor reduces liabilities — salaries, pensions, interest payments on past borrowings, subsidies, administrative costs, and grants to states for current consumption. In Budget 2026–27, total government expenditure is ₹53,47,315 crore (7.7% higher than 2025–26 RE); interest payments alone = 26% of total expenditure and 40% of revenue receipts, highlighting the debt-servicing burden. Capital expenditure in 2026–27 is expected to rise ~11.5% over revised estimates of 2025–26. UPSC tests this with specific line items — "is PM Kisan payment capex or revex?" (RevEx — it is a subsidy/transfer, not asset creation); "is a grant to a state for building a school capex or revex?" (depends on grant type — if for capital creation it can be classified as grants-in-aid of capital nature).

**UPSC Traps:**
- **Trap 1:** All government spending on infrastructure is capital expenditure — FALSE; maintenance expenditure on existing roads/buildings is revenue expenditure
- **Trap 2:** Repayment of loans is not expenditure — FALSE; repayment of government's own borrowings is capital expenditure (it reduces a liability)
- **Trap 3:** Subsidies like food and fertiliser subsidies are capital expenditure because they "invest" in agriculture — FALSE; subsidies are always revenue expenditure regardless of their purpose

---

### 10. FRBM Act — Targets, Escape Clauses, Fiscal Council

The **Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act 2003** (amended in 2012, 2018) mandates fiscal discipline through quantitative deficit targets. The N.K. Singh Committee (2017) recommended a **fiscal consolidation path**: fiscal deficit to be reduced to 2.5% of GDP by 2022–23; however, COVID-19 disrupted these targets. The current framework sets a target of **4.5% of GDP** fiscal deficit by FY26 (under the revised 2021 fiscal rules), with the government now tracking toward 4.3% in 2026–27. The Act includes **escape clauses** — the government can deviate from targets by up to **0.5% of GDP** in years of national calamity, collapse of agricultural output, structural reforms, decline in real output growth below 3%, or national security emergencies. The Finance Secretary certifies such deviations. The Act also mandated the creation of a **Fiscal Council** (an independent body to review and advise on fiscal consolidation) — this has NOT yet been operationalised, remaining a persistent implementation gap. The **primary deficit** (not just fiscal deficit) is also a monitoring target. UPSC has tested the definition of sterilisation as an FRBM-adjacent concept and has asked about 15th Finance Commission recommendations in the context of fiscal devolution.

**UPSC Traps:**
- **Trap 1:** FRBM targets are constitutionally mandated — FALSE; FRBM is a statutory law (Parliamentary Act), not a constitutional provision; it can be suspended by Parliament
- **Trap 2:** The Fiscal Council under FRBM is operational and independently reviews government budgets — FALSE; the Fiscal Council has NOT been established despite being recommended; FRBM review is done internally
- **Trap 3:** Escape clause deviation requires Parliamentary approval in advance — FALSE; the government must explain the deviation in Parliament's budget documents but does not need prior Parliamentary sanction

### Concept 12: CPI vs WPI — What They Measure, Base Year, Who Releases

**Consumer Price Index (CPI)** measures the change in retail prices of a basket of goods and services consumed by urban and rural households. It is released by the **Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI)** on a monthly basis, with the **base year 2012=100**. CPI has four sub-indices: CPI-Rural, CPI-Urban, CPI-Industrial Workers (CPI-IW, base 2016, released by Labour Bureau), and the combined CPI. The **RBI uses CPI (Combined) as its primary inflation benchmark** for monetary policy decisions under the flexible inflation targeting framework — the target is **4% with a band of ±2% (i.e., 2%–6%)**. **Wholesale Price Index (WPI)** measures price changes at the producer/wholesale level, released by the **Office of the Economic Adviser, Ministry of Commerce and Industry**, with **base year 2011–12=100**. WPI covers three groups: Primary Articles (~22.6% weight), Fuel & Power (~13.1%), and Manufactured Products (~64.2%); it does not include services, making it structurally different from CPI. The **CPI food weight is ~39%** (combined), while WPI's primary food articles carry lower weight — this divergence often causes CPI and WPI to move in opposite directions during supply-side shocks.

**UPSC Traps:**
- Students confuse "who releases" — CPI is MoSPI, WPI is Ministry of Commerce; CPI-IW is Labour Bureau (different base year: 2016)
- RBI targets CPI, NOT WPI — a classic trap question framing asks which index the MPC uses
- WPI can be negative (deflation at wholesale) while CPI remains positive — both can coexist; PYQ 2023 tested this directional nuance

---

### Concept 13: Core Inflation vs Headline Inflation

**Headline inflation** is the total inflation measured by CPI or WPI, including all components — food, fuel, and core items. It is the figure reported in newspapers and used for RBI's statutory inflation target. **Core inflation** is headline inflation **minus food and fuel components** — it represents the "sticky" or "underlying" inflation driven by demand-side pressures, services pricing, and input cost pass-throughs. Core inflation is considered a better indicator of **durable inflationary trends** because food and fuel prices are volatile and supply-driven. In India, core CPI inflation has historically been stickier than headline — even when headline falls due to a good monsoon collapsing food prices, core may remain elevated, signalling persistent demand pressures. The **RBI's MPC often looks at core inflation** alongside headline to judge whether rate cuts are appropriate — cutting rates when core is high risks entrenching inflation. As of 2024–25, core CPI was approximately **3.5–3.7%**, while headline oscillated between 4.5–5.5% due to vegetable price volatility.

**UPSC Traps:**
- Core inflation ≠ WPI — it is a sub-component of CPI; don't conflate these
- "Core" excludes both food AND fuel — some MCQs offer options excluding only one of the two
- The RBI legally targets headline CPI, not core — but monitors core for policy signals; this distinction is testable

---

### Concept 14: Base Effect in Inflation Calculation

**Base effect** refers to the distortion in the year-on-year inflation rate caused by an unusually high or low price level in the base (comparison) period. If prices were abnormally high in the same month last year, the current year's inflation rate will appear lower even if absolute prices haven't changed — this is a **favourable base effect** (inflation looks deflated). Conversely, if prices were unusually low last year, current inflation appears higher — an **unfavourable base effect**. Base effect is critical to interpreting India's monthly WPI and CPI data, particularly around the COVID-19 period (2020–21 base was very low, making 2021–22 inflation appear elevated) and during food price spikes. The **RBI explicitly flags base effects** in its monetary policy statements to avoid misreading transient inflation spikes as durable. Base effect is mathematically represented as: **Inflation(t) = [(P(t) - P(t-12)) / P(t-12)] × 100** — so P(t-12) denominator directly determines the rate even when P(t) is unchanged. UPSC has tested base effect understanding through directional reasoning questions asking whether high past prices lead to lower measured inflation.

**UPSC Traps:**
- A "favourable base effect" means inflation appears lower — students wrongly assume "favourable" means prices are actually lower
- Base effect is a statistical phenomenon, not a real economic change — policy should not be tightened/loosened based on it alone
- The concept connects to WPI deflation in India (2014–16 period) — largely a base effect from falling global commodity prices

---

### Concept 15: GDP vs GNP vs NNP vs GVA — What Each Measures

**GDP (Gross Domestic Product)** measures the total market value of all final goods and services produced **within a country's borders** in a given year, regardless of who owns the factors of production. **GNP (Gross National Product)** = GDP + Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA) — it attributes output to **residents/nationals** regardless of where they are located; if Indian workers send remittances home, this adds to GNP but not GDP. **NNP (Net National Product)** = GNP − Depreciation (Capital Consumption Allowance) — it is the most accurate measure of a nation's net addition to wealth after accounting for wear and tear of capital. **NNP at Factor Cost = National Income** in India's accounting framework. **GVA (Gross Value Added)** = Output − Intermediate Consumption; it measures value created at each stage of production. The relationship is: **GDP (at market prices) = GVA (at basic prices) + Product Taxes − Product Subsidies**. India's GDP estimation uses the **expenditure method** for headline reporting, while GVA sectoral data uses the **production/value-added method**. MoSPI is the official agency; base year is **2011–12**.

**UPSC Traps:**
- GDP > GNP when a country has more foreign nationals working domestically than its own nationals abroad (like a major FDI destination); India's GNP > GDP due to large diaspora remittances
- "National Income" in India specifically = NNP at Factor Cost — don't use it loosely
- The 2025 PYQ content explicitly tests GDP_MP = GVA_BP + taxes − subsidies formula; this is a direct trap

---

### Concept 16: Real GDP vs Nominal GDP — Deflator, Purchasing Power Parity

**Nominal GDP** values current year output at current year prices — it can rise simply because prices increased, not because actual production increased. **Real GDP** adjusts for inflation by valuing current output at **base year prices (2011–12)**, thus reflecting genuine changes in production volume. The **GDP Deflator** = (Nominal GDP / Real GDP) × 100 — it is a broader price index than CPI because it covers all goods/services in the economy, not just a consumer basket. When comparing GDP across countries, **Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)** adjusts for price level differences — $1 buys more in India than in the USA, so PPP-adjusted GDP is higher than exchange-rate-based GDP for low-cost countries. **India's GDP at PPP is approximately $13–14 trillion** (making it 3rd largest globally), while **nominal GDP at market exchange rates is ~$3.9 trillion** (5th largest). Real GDP growth in India for 2024–25 is estimated at **6.4%** (First Advance Estimate, MoSPI), while nominal GDP growth was higher at approximately 9.7% due to price increases.

**UPSC Traps:**
- GDP Deflator ≠ CPI — Deflator covers investment, government spending, and exports too; CPI covers only consumer basket
- India's ranking changes dramatically with PPP vs market exchange rate — UPSC tests this distinction
- "Real growth" means volume growth; positive nominal but negative real GDP = stagflation signal

### Concept 17: MSP — Who Recommends, Who Announces, How Computed (A2, FL, C2)

**Minimum Support Price (MSP)** is the floor price guaranteed by the Government of India to farmers to protect them against price crashes. The **Commission for Agricultural Costs and Prices (CACP)** recommends MSP for **23 mandated crops** (14 Kharif + 6 Rabi + 2 other commercial crops + copra). The **Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs (CCEA)** approves and announces the final MSP — not CACP, not the Ministry of Agriculture alone. CACP uses three cost concepts: **A2** (actual paid-out costs — seeds, fertilisers, hired labour, irrigation); **A2+FL** (A2 plus imputed value of family labour); and **C2** (A2+FL plus imputed rent on owned land and interest on owned capital — the most comprehensive). Since 2018, the government announced MSP at **at least 1.5 times the A2+FL cost** (not C2) — farmer organisations have consistently demanded MSP at 1.5× C2. The **PM-AASHA scheme** (2018) supplements MSP by providing price deficiency payments and procurement. The 2025 Economic Survey noted turmeric and other spice crops in the context of MSP extensions.

**UPSC Traps:**
- CACP recommends but does NOT announce — CCEA announces; a standard trap since 2019
- The 50% return above cost applies to A2+FL cost, not C2 — C2 gives higher cost base and is demanded by farmers
- MSP is NOT a legal entitlement under current law — the government is not legally bound to procure at MSP from all farmers; this was central to the 2020–21 farm law controversy

---

### Concept 18: Procurement vs Open Market Availability — FCI's Dual Role

The **Food Corporation of India (FCI)**, established under the Food Corporations Act 1964, performs a dual function: **procurement** (buying rice and wheat at MSP from farmers, primarily Punjab, Haryana, UP, MP) and **distribution** (supplying grain to states under the National Food Security Act/NFSA for PDS/PMGKAY). FCI also maintains **buffer stocks** — strategic reserves that act as food security insurance. The government-mandated **buffer stock norms** set minimum stockholding levels; as of 2025, actual stocks often far exceed norms, creating carrying costs. For releasing grain into open markets to stabilise retail prices (combat inflation), the government uses the **Open Market Sales Scheme (OMSS)** — FCI sells grain directly to bulk buyers, state governments, and flour mills below market price. PMGKAY (PM Garib Kalyan Anna Yojana) was made permanent under NFSA from January 2024, providing **5 kg free grain per person per month** to ~81 crore beneficiaries, significantly impacting FCI's off-take and procurement economics.

**UPSC Traps:**
- FCI procurement ≠ MSP announcement — FCI buys at MSP but the price is set by CCEA on CACP recommendation
- OMSS is the open market tool for price stabilisation — not MSP procurement; these operate in opposite directions
- Buffer stock norms are revised periodically; the government is not obligated to procure beyond norms — strategic release through OMSS controls price inflation

### Concept 19: e-NAM — How It Works, Who Manages, APMC Reform Linkage

**e-NAM (Electronic National Agriculture Market)** is a pan-India online trading portal launched in **April 2016** that networks existing **APMC (Agricultural Produce Market Committee) mandis** to create a unified national market for agricultural commodities. It is managed by the **Small Farmers' Agribusiness Consortium (SFAC)**, a Government of India entity under the Ministry of Agriculture. As of 2024–25, **e-NAM covers 1,389+ mandis** across 23 states and 3 UTs, with over 1.77 crore farmers and 2.5 lakh traders registered. The platform enables **price discovery through competitive online bidding** — farmers or commission agents upload produce quality/assay data, and buyers across the country bid electronically, reducing intermediary layers. The critical linkage is with **APMC reform**: states must amend their APMC Acts to allow inter-mandi trading within e-NAM; without APMC reform, e-NAM's national integration is limited. The **Model APMC Act and the 2020 Farm Laws** (later repealed) both attempted to remove mandatory APMC levies for e-NAM to function seamlessly across state borders.

**UPSC Traps:**
- e-NAM does NOT bypass APMCs — it works through them; it's a networking tool, not a replacement
- SFAC manages e-NAM, not FCI, not NABARD — this specific institutional detail has been tested
- e-NAM's success is contingent on states' willingness to reform APMC Acts — without legal reform, the platform's inter-state price discovery is constrained

### Concept 20: PM-KISAN — Eligibility, Exclusions, Amount, DBT Mechanism

**PM-KISAN (Pradhan Mantri Kisan Samman Nidhi)** provides income support of **₹6,000 per year** (in three equal instalments of ₹2,000 each, every four months) directly to **landholder farmer families** through **Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT)** to Aadhaar-linked bank accounts. Launched in **February 2019**, it covers all farmer families owning cultivable land, subject to exclusions. **Exclusions include**: institutional landholders; farmer families with one or more members who are/were income taxpayers; current/former holders of Constitutional posts; serving/retired officers of Central/State Government (Class I/II); multi-tasking staff/Group D/Chowkidar excluded after 2019 revision. Professionals like doctors, engineers, lawyers, CAs, and architects are also excluded. As of 2024–25, over **11 crore farmers** receive benefits; total outlay since inception exceeds ₹3.45 lakh crore. The scheme uses the **PM-KISAN portal** integrated with the land records database — e-KYC via Aadhaar is mandatory. **PMDDKY (PM Dhan-Dhaanya Krishi Yojana)**, announced in Budget 2025–26, consolidates 36 agricultural schemes and works in convergence with PM-KISAN.

**UPSC Traps:**
- PM-KISAN is for landholder farmers only — **tenant farmers and sharecroppers are NOT covered** under the original scheme (a persistent trap)
- The ₹6,000 is annual, not monthly — ₹2,000 per instalment; confusing this is a standard MCQ trick
- DBT via Aadhaar-linked accounts is the delivery mechanism — the government saved crores by eliminating ghost beneficiaries through this integration

### Concept 21: PMFBY (Crop Insurance) — Premium Sharing, Actuarial Premium

**Pradhan Mantri Fasal Bima Yojana (PMFBY)**, launched in **January 2016**, provides comprehensive crop insurance at low farmer premiums with the government sharing the remaining actuarial cost. Farmer premium rates are: **2% of sum insured for Kharif crops**, **1.5% for Rabi crops**, and **5% for annual commercial/horticultural crops** — the **actuarial premium** (the technically correct insurance cost based on historical losses) is typically much higher (10–20%). The difference between the actuarial premium and farmer premium is shared between **Central Government (50%) and State Government (50%)** for non-NE states; for **Northeast and hilly states**, the Centre bears **90%**. The scheme covers **prevented sowing, mid-season adversity, post-harvest losses (up to 14 days), and localised calamities**. Implementation was revamped in 2020 — it became **voluntary** for all farmers (previously mandatory for KCC holders), and state governments can choose to opt in or out. **RWBCIS (Restructured Weather Based Crop Insurance Scheme)** runs parallel to PMFBY. Actual premium data is uploaded to the **National Crop Insurance Portal**.

**UPSC Traps:**
- Premium rates vary by season — 2% (Kharif) ≠ 1.5% (Rabi) ≠ 5% (commercial); options in MCQs often swap these
- Centre:State premium sharing is 50:50 for most states — but 90:10 for NE/hilly states; this distinction is tested
- Voluntary from 2020 — previously compulsory for loanee farmers; this change is a direct PYQ trigger

### Concept 22: PLI (Production Linked Incentive) — How It Works, Sectors, Budget 2025 Updates

**Production Linked Incentive (PLI)** scheme provides financial incentives to manufacturers based on **incremental sales from base year turnover** — firms receive a percentage of incremental sales as a direct cash incentive over 4–6 years, encouraging both domestic production and exports. The scheme was announced for **14 key sectors** with a total outlay of approximately **₹1.97 lakh crore** over five years. Key sectors include: Mobile phones and electronic components (largest at ~₹41,000 crore), Pharmaceuticals, Medical Devices, Automobiles and Auto Components, Advanced Chemistry Cell Batteries, Textile Products, Food Processing, Telecom & Networking Products, White Goods (ACs and LEDs), Specialty Steel, Solar PV Modules, and others. The **Budget 2025–26** continued PLI support and announced enhancements for electronics manufacturing (including laptops and servers) as part of the broader "Make in India" and Atmanirbhar Bharat agenda. PLI has been credited with a significant boost in mobile phone exports — India became the **2nd largest mobile phone manufacturer globally** after China by 2023–24, with Apple's iPhone exports crossing ₹1.5 lakh crore.

**UPSC Traps:**
- PLI is NOT a subsidy on inputs — it incentivises output/sales increments; this mechanism distinction matters
- 14 sectors are covered but the outlay varies enormously by sector — mobile/electronics dominates
- PLI companies must meet minimum investment thresholds AND sales targets — failure to meet either disqualifies the incentive for that year

### Concept 23: IBC (Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code) — NCLT, NCLAT, Timelines, Waterfall

**Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016** created a unified, time-bound insolvency resolution framework replacing the earlier fragmented system (SICA, BIFR, DRT). The **Corporate Insolvency Resolution Process (CIRP)** must be completed within **180 days**, extendable by **90 days** (total 270 days) with the NCLT's permission; a further extension was provided to 330 days by a 2019 amendment including legal proceedings. The adjudicating authority for corporate insolvency is **NCLT (National Company Law Tribunal)**; appeals go to **NCLAT (National Company Law Appellate Tribunal)**; further appeals go to the Supreme Court. For individuals and firms, the adjudicating authority is the **Debt Recovery Tribunal (DRT)**. The **waterfall mechanism** (priority of claims) under Section 53: (**1**) Insolvency Resolution/Liquidation costs, (**2**) Secured creditors + workmen dues (24 months), (**3**) Other employee dues (12 months), (**4**) Unsecured creditors, (**5**) Government dues, (**6**) Remaining secured creditors, (**7**) Equity shareholders. The **Insolvency and Bankruptcy Board of India (IBBI)** is the regulator. Recovery rate under IBC has improved from ~26 cents per dollar pre-IBC to approximately 32–35 cents.

**UPSC Traps:**
- NCLT handles corporate insolvency; DRT handles individual/partnership insolvency — mixing these is the primary trap
- Timeline is 180+90 = 270 days (legally) but 330 days including court time — the specific numbers appear in MCQ options
- Waterfall priority: secured creditors BEFORE government dues — this reversal from intuition (that government should rank higher) is specifically testable

---

### Concept 24: MUDRA Loans — Shishu/Kishore/Tarun Tiers, Who Lends, Collateral Rule

**MUDRA (Micro Units Development and Refinance Agency)** was established in 2015 as a subsidiary of SIDBI under the **Pradhan Mantri MUDRA Yojana (PMMY)**. MUDRA does NOT directly lend to borrowers — it **refinances** commercial banks, RRBs, MFIs, and NBFCs who in turn lend to non-corporate small business units (micro-enterprises). Three loan tiers: **Shishu** (up to ₹50,000 — for very early-stage businesses), **Kishore** (₹50,001 to ₹5 lakh — for established micro-enterprises), and **Tarun** (₹5 lakh to ₹10 lakh — for expanding micro-enterprises). A **₹20 lakh limit (Tarun Plus)** was introduced in Budget 2024–25 for entrepreneurs who have successfully repaid Tarun category loans, enabling graduation. **No collateral** is required for MUDRA loans — this is a defining feature. As of 2024–25, cumulative sanctions exceed **₹33 lakh crore** with over **47 crore loan accounts**. Women borrowers constitute approximately 68% of MUDRA loan beneficiaries.

**UPSC Traps:**
- MUDRA is a refinancer, NOT a direct lender — the actual loans come from banks/MFIs/NBFCs; this is a consistent UPSC trap
- Shishu ≤ ₹50,000; Kishore ₹50K–5L; Tarun ₹5L–10L — MCQs often swap these thresholds
- No collateral requirement is statutory for MUDRA — some options suggest "collateral-free only for Shishu," which is wrong

### Concept 25: Jan Dhan Yojana — Key Features, Overdraft, DBT Linkage

**Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana (PMJDY)**, launched on **28 August 2014**, is the world's largest financial inclusion initiative. Key features: zero-balance savings accounts, **RuPay debit card** with inbuilt accidental insurance cover of ₹1 lakh (enhanced to ₹2 lakh for accounts opened after August 2018), **overdraft facility of ₹10,000** (enhanced from ₹5,000 in 2018) for accounts with satisfactory transaction history (previously ₹5,000 only after 6 months; now available immediately to one account per household, preferably the lady of the household), life cover of ₹30,000 under PMJJBY linkage. As of 2025, over **54 crore Jan Dhan accounts** are operational with deposits exceeding ₹2.3 lakh crore. The scheme is the **backbone of the JAM Trinity** (Jan Dhan–Aadhaar–Mobile) enabling DBT for PM-KISAN, LPG subsidy (PAHAL), scholarships, and MGNREGA wages. The government claims **₹3.48 lakh crore saved** through DBT and plug of ghost beneficiaries between 2014 and 2024.

**UPSC Traps:**
- Overdraft limit is ₹10,000 (NOT ₹5,000 — that was pre-2018) — outdated figures frequently appear as distractors
- Accidental insurance is ₹2 lakh for accounts post-August 2018 — older accounts have ₹1 lakh; this nuance is testable
- Jan Dhan accounts are with **commercial banks and RRBs** — NOT post offices alone; some options restrict it to post offices

### Concept 26: FDI vs FPI/FII — Definitions, Automatic vs Approval Route, Sectoral Caps

**Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)** involves acquiring a lasting management interest (typically **≥10% equity stake** in a company) — it represents long-term investment in productive assets (factories, infrastructure). **Foreign Portfolio Investment (FPI)** involves investment in financial securities (equity <10%, bonds) without management control — it is inherently more volatile and reversible. **Automatic Route** allows FDI up to permitted limits without prior government approval (RBI post-facto reporting suffices); **Government/Approval Route** requires prior FIPB/inter-ministerial clearance (FIPB was abolished in 2017; approvals now through respective ministries). Sectors with **100% FDI under Automatic Route**: Manufacturing, IT, most services. Sectors with **caps or Government Route**: Telecom (100%, was 74% automatically), Defence (74% automatic; beyond that, Government Route), Multi-brand retail (51%, Government Route), Broadcasting (49%), Banking-private (74%), Insurance (74% after 2021 amendment). **FDI into China+1 beneficiary countries** — India attracted record FDI of ~$71 billion in FY23 before moderating to ~$47 billion in FY24 due to global uncertainties.

**UPSC Traps:**
- 10% threshold distinguishes FDI from FPI — below 10% = FPI even if in equity; above 10% = classified as FDI
- FIPB was abolished in 2017 — questions still frame options around FIPB approval; this is now defunct
- Multi-brand retail at 51% requires Government Route AND state government consent — the dual conditionality is tested

### Concept 27: Masala Bonds — Who Issues, Currency, Denomination

**Masala Bonds** are rupee-denominated bonds issued by **Indian entities in overseas markets** — the term "masala" was coined by the IFC (International Finance Corporation) evoking Indian culture. They were first issued by IFC itself in 2014 on the London Stock Exchange to raise funds for Indian infrastructure projects. Key feature: they are denominated in **Indian Rupees (INR)** but settled in foreign currency — this means the **currency risk is borne by the foreign investor**, not the Indian issuer (unlike External Commercial Borrowings in dollars where Indian borrower bears the risk). They can be issued by **corporates, banks, and the Indian government** (via NTPC, HDFC, etc.). Masala Bonds must list on recognised overseas exchanges (London, Singapore). **Minimum maturity**: 3 years for amounts ≥ ₹50 crore equivalent; no minimum for smaller amounts (1 year). They fall under **ECB framework** regulated by RBI. The proceeds can be used for most purposes except land purchase and real estate.

**UPSC Traps:**
- Currency risk is with the **foreign investor** in Masala Bonds — opposite of dollar-denominated ECBs where the Indian borrower bears forex risk; this reversal is the primary trap
- Masala Bonds are NOT Sovereign Bonds — they are issued by Indian companies/entities, not the Government of India directly
- "Rupee-denominated" ≠ "raised domestically" — they are issued in overseas markets but denominated in rupees

### Concept 28: Green Bonds / Sovereign Green Bonds — India's Issuance, Use of Proceeds

**Sovereign Green Bonds (SGrBs)** are government securities issued to raise funds specifically for **green infrastructure projects** — the proceeds must be used for eligible green expenditures as defined in India's **Sovereign Green Bond Framework (2022)**. India issued its first Sovereign Green Bonds in **January 2023**, raising ₹16,000 crore in two tranches (₹8,000 crore each) — part of the government's fiscal 2022–23 borrowing programme. The **Framework** specifies eligible categories: renewable energy (solar, wind), energy efficiency, clean transportation, sustainable water management, pollution control, green buildings, and sustainable agriculture. The Ministry of Finance issues SGrBs; proceeds are **ring-fenced in a Public Account** (Green Fund) and cannot be used for general expenditure. India's SGrBs are priced at a **"greenium"** (green premium — slightly lower yield than comparable conventional G-Secs) signalling market appetite. In Budget 2025–26, the government continued SGrB issuance as part of its **net-zero by 2070** financing strategy. RBI manages the issuance through the usual auction mechanism.

**UPSC Traps:**
- SGrBs are part of the **government's market borrowing programme** — they don't represent additional borrowing beyond the budgeted fiscal deficit
- "Green bonds" issued by corporates/banks are different from **Sovereign Green Bonds** — UPSC may conflate the two in question framing
- Greenium is the LOWER yield on green bonds vs conventional bonds (not higher) — investors accept lower returns for environmental credibility

### Concept 29: T-Bills vs G-Secs vs SDL — Tenure, Issuer, Who Can Buy

**Treasury Bills (T-Bills)** are short-term money market instruments issued by the **Central Government** through RBI auctions, with **three tenures: 91-day, 182-day, and 364-day**. They are **zero-coupon instruments** — issued at a discount and redeemed at face value; no interim interest payments. **Government Securities (G-Secs)** are long-term debt instruments issued by the Central Government with **tenures from 5 to 40 years**, paying semi-annual coupons; the benchmark 10-year G-Sec yield is India's risk-free rate reference (approximately **6.6–6.8%** in 2024–25). **State Development Loans (SDLs)** are G-Sec equivalents issued by **State Governments** through RBI auctions — they carry a spread (~25–75 bps) over Central G-Secs. All three can be purchased by: banks (for SLR compliance), insurance companies (IRDA mandated), provident funds, FPIs (within limits), mutual funds, and **retail investors** through the **RBI Retail Direct Scheme** (launched 2021). **Floating Rate Bonds, Inflation-Indexed Bonds, and Sovereign Gold Bonds** are special categories of G-Secs.

**UPSC Traps:**
- T-Bills are issued ONLY by the Central Government — States issue SDLs (also called State G-Secs), not T-Bills
- T-Bills are zero-coupon; G-Secs pay coupons — the interest structure differs fundamentally
- SDLs carry a spread over Central G-Secs — they are NOT the same price/yield as Central government securities

### Concept 30: Current Account vs Capital Account in BOP — What Goes Where

**Balance of Payments (BOP)** is a systematic record of all economic transactions between residents of a country and the rest of the world over a year. The **Current Account** records: (a) trade in goods (merchandise exports − imports = **trade balance**); (b) trade in services (software exports, tourism, shipping); (c) primary income (compensation of employees, investment income — interest, dividends); (d) secondary income (**remittances** — India's largest component, crossing $125 billion in 2023–24). The **Capital Account** in India's BOP is small and records: capital transfers (debt forgiveness, migrants' transfers) and acquisition/disposal of non-produced, non-financial assets. The **Financial Account** records: FDI, FPI, ECB, NRI deposits, forex reserve changes, and banking capital. Note: IMF's BOP Manual 6 distinguishes Capital Account and Financial Account separately — India follows this; older texts club them. **BOP always balances** due to double-entry bookkeeping — any current account deficit is financed by the capital/financial account surplus or reserve drawdown.

**UPSC Traps:**
- Remittances go in **Current Account** (secondary income) — not Capital Account, despite the word "capital" suggesting capital flows
- FDI, FPI, ECBs go in the **Financial Account** — not Capital Account; the nomenclature is confusing but precise
- "BOP crisis" means inability to fund current account deficit through financial inflows — reserves fall dangerously; 1991 is India's reference event

### Concept 31: CAD (Current Account Deficit) — Components, India's Typical CAD

**Current Account Deficit (CAD)** occurs when a country's imports of goods, services, and transfers exceed its exports — it means the country is a net borrower from the world. India **structurally runs a CAD** due to: (a) large merchandise trade deficit (oil imports, gold, electronics > exports); (b) partially offset by services surplus (IT/BPO exports) and remittances. India's **merchandise trade deficit** is typically **$200–250 billion annually** (FY24: ~$238 billion). The services surplus is ~$140 billion; net remittances ~$100 billion — these two together offset much of the trade deficit. India's CAD for **FY24 was ~$23.2 billion (0.7% of GDP)** — relatively comfortable. For **FY23, CAD was $67 billion (2.0% of GDP)** due to oil price spike. The **sustainable level of CAD** for India is considered ~2.5% of GDP — beyond this, currency depreciation pressure intensifies. Financing CAD requires FDI + FPI + ECB inflows; if these dry up (capital flight), forex reserves deplete.

**UPSC Traps:**
- Trade deficit ≠ CAD — trade deficit is merchandise only; CAD includes services and transfers; India's CAD is much smaller than its trade deficit because of service exports and remittances
- Remittances REDUCE CAD (they are credit items in current account) — a common misconception that remittances = capital flows
- The 2023 PYQ concept linking "capital flight" to firm borrowing costs (ECB interest costs) is directly related to CAD financing — when CAD is high and capital flees, ECB rollovers become expensive

### Concept 32: NEER vs REER — What Depreciation Actually Means

**NEER (Nominal Effective Exchange Rate)** is a weighted average of bilateral exchange rates of the domestic currency against a basket of trading partner currencies, using **trade weights** — it captures the overall nominal strength/weakness of the currency without adjusting for inflation. **REER (Real Effective Exchange Rate)** adjusts NEER for relative inflation between India and its trading partners: **REER = NEER × (Domestic Price Level / Foreign Price Level)** — it indicates competitiveness. An **increase in NEER/REER = appreciation** (currency stronger, exports relatively more expensive). A **decrease = depreciation** (currency weaker, exports cheaper, imports more expensive). **RBI publishes REER with base 2015–16=100** for a **36-currency basket**. If India's inflation is higher than trading partners but the nominal exchange rate is unchanged, REER appreciates even though NEER hasn't changed — meaning India's export competitiveness deteriorates in real terms. India's REER has historically been close to 100–105 indicating relative competitiveness.

**UPSC Traps:**
- REER appreciation can happen even without nominal rupee appreciation — if domestic inflation > foreign inflation; this is the key UPSC concept
- "Rupee depreciation helps exports" is an NEER story — in REER terms, depreciation helps only if it exceeds inflation differential
- RBI uses both NEER and REER for exchange rate assessment but neither is an explicit policy target (unlike inflation targeting)

---

### Concept 33: GST — CGST/SGST/IGST Structure, GST Council Voting, Compensation Cess

**Goods and Services Tax (GST)** is a destination-based, multi-stage, comprehensive indirect tax implemented from **1 July 2017**. Structure: **CGST** (Central GST) and **SGST** (State GST) apply on intra-state supplies — revenue split between Centre and States; **IGST** (Integrated GST) applies on inter-state supplies — Centre collects and apportions to the destination state. **UTGST** applies in Union Territories. GST rates: **0%, 5%, 12%, 18%, 28%** — with a **Compensation Cess** on luxury and sin goods (cars, tobacco, aerated drinks) atop the 28% slab. The **GST Council** is chaired by the Union Finance Minister with all state Finance Ministers as members — **voting: Centre has 1/3 weight; States collectively have 2/3 weight; decisions need 3/4 majority** (i.e., Centre must vote for any decision to pass, giving it effective veto). **Compensation to states** (for GST revenue shortfall vs guaranteed 14% growth over 2015–16 base) ended **June 2022** — the five-year compensation period concluded. The Compensation Cess was extended to **March 2026** to repay back-to-back loans taken during COVID.

**UPSC Traps:**
- GST Council voting: Centre has 1/3, States 2/3, and 3/4 required — this means Centre's assent is mathematically necessary (1/3 > 1/4 shortfall); tested directly
- Compensation Cess ended in June 2022 but the cess itself continues until March 2026 for loan repayment — these are two different end-dates
- IGST is NOT collected by both Centre and States — Centre alone collects IGST and then apportions to destination state

### Concept 34: Direct vs Indirect Tax — Incidence vs Impact, Progressive vs Regressive

**Direct taxes** are those where the **impact (statutory liability) and incidence (actual economic burden) fall on the same person** — examples: Income Tax, Corporate Tax, Capital Gains Tax, Securities Transaction Tax. They cannot be shifted to another party. **Indirect taxes** have the impact on one party (manufacturer/seller) but incidence is shifted to the consumer through higher prices — examples: GST, Customs Duty, Excise. Direct taxes are typically **progressive** (higher income → higher rate) while indirect taxes tend to be **regressive** (consume a larger share of poor people's income since everyone pays the same rate regardless of income). India's **tax-to-GDP ratio is approximately 10.8%** (Central taxes only, FY25 estimate); including states, it is ~18%. **Direct tax collections (₹22.4 lakh crore) now exceed indirect tax collections (₹16.6 lakh crore)** at the Central level in FY25, reflecting income/corporate tax buoyancy. The **Budget 2025–26 revised income tax slabs** under the new regime: ₹0–4L (nil), ₹4–8L (5%), ₹8–12L (10%), ₹12–16L (15%), ₹16–20L (20%), ₹20–24L (25%), above ₹24L (30%).

**UPSC Traps:**
- STT (Securities Transaction Tax) is a DIRECT tax — it cannot be shifted; many students classify it as indirect
- "Incidence" and "impact" — for indirect taxes, they diverge; for direct taxes, they coincide; this vocabulary distinction is directly tested
- GST on essential commodities at 0–5% is less regressive than older VAT/excise systems — UPSC tests whether students understand that GST's progressive rate structure partially mitigates regressivity

---

### Concept 35: Tax Buoyancy — Definition, India's Target

**Tax buoyancy** measures the **responsiveness of tax revenue to changes in GDP** — calculated as: **Tax Buoyancy = % Change in Tax Revenue / % Change in GDP**. A buoyancy of >1 means tax revenue grows faster than the economy (the tax system automatically captures economic growth efficiently); <1 means taxes grow slower than GDP (inefficient system). India targets a **tax buoyancy of ~1.2–1.5** for direct taxes, reflecting formalisation of the economy, GST's broadening of the indirect tax base, and improved compliance through technology (faceless assessment, TDS widening, AIS/TIS annual information statements). **GST's introduction dramatically improved indirect tax buoyancy** by bringing services and previously exempt goods into the tax net. **Direct tax buoyancy improved significantly post-2017** due to: IBC improving corporate governance, demonetisation-driven bank account formalisation, and mandatory PAN-Aadhaar linkage. For FY25, net direct tax collection growth is estimated at ~15% against nominal GDP growth of ~9.7% — implying buoyancy of ~1.5. The **FRBM Act's medium-term objective** targets tax-to-GDP improvement as part of fiscal consolidation.

**UPSC Traps:**
- Buoyancy >1 is DESIRABLE (not a sign of over-taxation) — it means the tax system efficiently captures economic growth
- Tax buoyancy ≠ tax elasticity — elasticity holds tax rates constant; buoyancy includes both rate changes AND base expansion; UPSC occasionally tests this distinction
- GST improved buoyancy for the Centre AND states simultaneously — this is the political economy rationale for states accepting GST despite initial revenue risk

# SECTION 5: HOW UPSC TESTS ECONOMICS

UPSC's economics questions in Prelims are fundamentally tests of **conceptual precision under adversarial framing** — not recall of isolated facts. The examiner deliberately places correct-sounding but technically wrong statements alongside genuinely correct ones, exploiting three cognitive vulnerabilities.

**First, directional confusion.** The 2023–2024 questions on RBI operations (repo rate increase → banks borrow less from RBI → credit contraction) require students to trace a two-to-three step causal chain correctly. Getting the direction wrong — for instance, confusing OMO purchase (liquidity injection) with OMO sale (liquidity absorption) — is the designed trap. Similarly, the 2025 RBI income sources question requires knowing that lending to private companies is NOT an RBI function, exploiting students' intuition that "banks lend, so RBI lends too."

**Second, institutional attribution errors.** The 2025 capital receipts question (correct: Statements I and II only — borrowings create liability; interest received does NOT create liability) tests whether students have mapped each transaction to its correct accounting category. UPSC consistently places the "wrong institution" — CACP versus CCEA for MSP, MUDRA versus banks for loans, SFAC versus NABARD for e-NAM — as a trap option.

**Third, threshold-specific traps.** The RTGS/NEFT question exploited the fact that RTGS has minimum ₹2 lakh threshold (NEFT has none), that RTGS operates 24×7 but on stated holidays restrictions apply differently — making Statement III (RTGS restricted on certain days, NEFT not) the counterintuitive correct answer. The study methodology this demands: for every scheme, memorise the **specific number + the institutional owner + the one surprising exception**, because that exception is the question.

The optimal approach is **"statement elimination with causal reasoning"** — test each statement independently for both factual accuracy and logical direction, then use answer combinations to triangulate. Pure memorisation without mechanism understanding consistently fails on the directional and classification traps UPSC deploys.
